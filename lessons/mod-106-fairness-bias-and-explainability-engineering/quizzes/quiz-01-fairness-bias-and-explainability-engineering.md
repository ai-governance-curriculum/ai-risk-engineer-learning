# Quiz 01 — Fairness, Bias, and Explainability Engineering

**Format:** 12 multiple-choice questions · **Open-book.** Use the chapters as reference.

Each question tests a *concept* from the module, not phrasing. The answer key at the bottom cites the chapter the answer comes from.

---

## 1. A team runs Fairlearn on a hiring-shortlist classifier, sees demographic-parity difference at 0.03 and equalised-odds difference at 0.11, and writes the fairness conclusion against demographic parity because "the number is smaller." A reviewer flags the report as not-shippable. Per chapter 01, what specifically did the team get wrong, and what is the shippable shape?

- A. Nothing — demographic parity is the default headline and the smaller number is appropriate.
- B. They should have reported equalised odds because its number is larger; the larger-number metric is always the honest headline.
- C. They committed **metric roulette** — picking the metric after seeing the numbers. Which metric headlines is a *policy decision* that must be anchored to the harm model and the regulator, not a technical decision driven by whichever number flatters the model. The shippable shape is the **metric-choice five-liner** (decision context, protected-attribute partition, primary harm, headline metric, metrics explicitly not headlined) pinned *before* the evaluation is authored. For a US hiring shortlist the headline is disparate impact / four-fifths per the EEOC Uniform Guidelines, with equal opportunity as a diagnostic; demographic parity is not the right headline for that context.
- D. The team should run both metrics and average them.

## 2. A team's eval shows base rates of 42 % positives in the privileged group and 24 % positives in the unprivileged group. The team proposes a single classifier that simultaneously satisfies **predictive parity** *and* **equalised odds** to "cover both ProPublica's and Northpointe's objections at once." Per chapter 01, what does the chapter say is wrong with this plan?

- A. Nothing — both metrics can always be satisfied with enough tuning.
- B. **Chouldechova (2016)** proved that predictive parity and equalised odds (equal FPR *and* equal FNR) cannot both hold simultaneously when base rates differ across groups. The base rates here differ (42 % vs 24 %), so no classifier can satisfy both; the two sides of the ProPublica / Northpointe debate are mathematically irreconcilable at unequal base rates. The engineering shape is to pick a side — anchored to the harm model — and name the metric you are *not* headlining in the metric-choice five-liner, not to pretend both can be satisfied.
- C. The team should use demographic parity instead, which satisfies both.
- D. The team should equalise the base rates first and then both metrics will hold.

## 3. An engineer argues that since Fairlearn and AIF360 "both do fairness," picking one and standardising on it will save maintenance. Per chapter 02, what does the chapter say is the problem with the "pick one" stance, and what is the shippable split?

- A. Nothing — Fairlearn is strictly a superset of AIF360 and should replace it.
- B. AIF360 is strictly a superset of Fairlearn and should replace it.
- C. Neither is a superset of the other. Chapter 02's reach-for table pins Fairlearn as the assessment / reporting workhorse (`MetricFrame` produces the per-group table that drops straight into a report; intersectional slicing is one call; smallest dependency footprint) and AIF360 as the algorithm zoo where specific named-paper mitigation algorithms live (Reweighing by Kamiran & Calders; Adversarial Debiasing by Zhang et al.; Calibrated Equalised Odds Post-Processing by Pleiss et al.; and so on). The shippable pattern is to use **both** — Fairlearn for the primary assessment and reporting, AIF360 for whichever mitigation paper the chapter-04 table points you at.
- D. Pick whichever has the better documentation this quarter.

## 4. An evaluation ships a per-group table where the `race=Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander × sex=Female` cell has `n=11` with a reported selection-rate disparity of 0.47 vs the privileged group. The team *deletes* the row from the final report "to avoid confusing readers with low-sample noise." Per chapter 02, what are the two specific things wrong with that choice?

- A. Nothing — deleting low-sample cells is the recommended practice.
- B. First, **chapter 02 is explicit that low-power cells are flagged, not deleted** — an auditor will ask why a cell disappeared and the report cannot defend the removal. The correct treatment is to keep the row, carry the per-cell count, attach a Wilson 95 % CI (which will be wide and legitimately so), and mark the cell `low-power` with a caveat on the metric value. Second, the report must also give **intersectional slices** per chapter 02's requirement — the deletion specifically removes the intersectional view that LL144 and the chapter-02 slice-report shape require. A "single-axis only" report plus deleted intersectional cells is the exact anti-pattern pair the chapter names.
- C. The report should delete the cell and increase the sample size on a re-run; that is the only acceptable fix.
- D. The report should replace the row with imputed values from the single-axis marginals.

## 5. A team runs individual-fairness nearest-neighbour consistency with a similarity metric `d` built from the model's full input feature set — including `postcode`, `school attended`, and `prior employer` — and reports that the classifier is individually fair because `d`-close individuals get consistent predictions. Per chapter 03, what does the chapter say is wrong with this certificate?

- A. Nothing — individual fairness under any `d` is a valid certificate.
- B. The engineer should have used a larger `k` for the nearest-neighbour search.
- C. **The similarity metric `d` is the whole game** — Dwork et al. are explicit that `d` is a task-specific input whose construction is where the ethical judgement lives. Here `d` includes features (`postcode`, `school`, `employer`) that are documented proxies for the protected attribute in US contexts; two individuals that are `d`-close under this metric are also close on the protected attribute by construction, so "similar individuals get similar predictions" certifies *consistency with the proxy structure*, which is the bias the fairness question is asking about. The chapter names this directly as an anti-pattern: "individual fairness with a `d` that encodes the bias." The fix is to construct `d` from features that *exclude* the protected attributes and known proxies and to cite the exclusion list in the report.
- D. Individual fairness should never be used; only group fairness is valid.

## 6. A team wants to ship a counterfactual-fairness diagnostic (Kusner et al.) for a loan-approval classifier. They draft a DAG from a lunchtime whiteboard session, do not run an identifiability check, fit a linear-Gaussian SCM, and compute counterfactual decisions. Per chapter 03, which of the following is the correct reading of this work?

- A. The diagnostic is complete — a DAG and an SCM fit is sufficient for counterfactual fairness.
- B. The diagnostic fails three preconditions chapter 03 names: a **defensible DAG** (one whose arrows you can cite from domain literature or domain-expert judgement — not whiteboard convenience), **identifiability** (a formal check that the counterfactual you want to compute is identified from the observational distribution under your DAG — not optional), and **a means to compute the counterfactual** (linear-Gaussian SCM is fine for a first pass, but it must be chosen deliberately, not defaulted to). A causal-fairness number ridden on an undefended DAG is the exact anti-pattern — chapter 03 calls a fabricated causal number *worse than no causal number*, because the undefended DAG gives the result the appearance of rigour without the substance. If the DAG is contested, the honest deliverable is a short "DAG not defensible" note that names the contested arrows and references the harm-model row that would need the diagnostic if the DAG became defensible later.
- C. The diagnostic is complete as long as the SCM is replaced with a probabilistic programme.
- D. The team should drop causal fairness entirely and use only group fairness.

## 7. A team runs the `holisticai` sweep on the running model, sees green across bias / explainability / robustness / privacy axes, and writes in the audit report that "the Holistic AI sweep confirms the model is trustworthy across all evaluated dimensions." Per chapter 03, what does the chapter say about this use of the sweep?

- A. The use is correct — a green Holistic AI sweep is a shippable trustworthiness conclusion.
- B. The team should have used AIF360 instead.
- C. The Holistic AI sweep is a **breadth scan** — one call deep per metric — whose value is surfacing the axis you would otherwise not have looked at. Chapter 03 is explicit that the sweep is *diagnostic, not headline* — its red flags become targeted-evaluation backlog items for Fairlearn / AIF360 / SHAP / Alibi, and its numbers do not ship as the audit conclusion. "The Holistic AI sweep confirms the model is trustworthy" is the sweep-as-conclusion anti-pattern the chapter names. The shippable framing is "the sweep flagged axes X and Y as red, which we then evaluated in depth with {Fairlearn / SHAP / Alibi} — here are those results" (or, if nothing flagged, "the sweep did not surface additional axes beyond the ones already evaluated in depth").
- D. Green on Holistic AI makes the per-axis depth evaluations unnecessary.

## 8. A team runs a single mitigation algorithm at a single hyperparameter setting, reports the new fairness number and the new overall accuracy, and claims the trade-off is resolved. A reviewer asks "what would happen at a different operating point, and what did per-group accuracy do?" Per chapter 04, what are the two specific things missing, and why does the chapter call the operating-point justification a load-bearing paragraph?

- A. Nothing — a single point is sufficient if the number is good.
- B. The reviewer is wrong; overall accuracy is the only accuracy number that matters.
- C. Two things are missing. First, chapter 04 requires the mitigation output to be a **Pareto curve, not a point** — sweep the mitigation's tuning parameter across at least three settings that span the interesting range, plot headline fairness against accuracy, mark the unmitigated baseline, and label the chosen operating point. Second, the Pareto table must carry **per-group accuracy at every point**, because a fairness win that comes from degrading the classifier for a specific group is not a win ("race to the bottom on accuracy" is the chapter-04 anti-pattern). The operating-point paragraph is load-bearing because it names *which* point was picked, *why that point and not its neighbours*, and what a reviewer preferring a neighbour point would argue — citing the harm-model row and the risk-appetite band. "We picked the middle point" is not a justification per chapter 04; neither is "it was best."
- D. The team should always pick the median point on the frontier.

## 9. A team ships a Fairlearn `ThresholdOptimizer` with per-group thresholds (tuned to satisfy `true_positive_rate_parity`) for a US employment-screening system. The serving path reads the applicant's race at decision time to select the threshold. Per chapter 04 (and chapter 06's jurisdictional note), what is the problem?

- A. Nothing — per-group thresholds are always deployable.
- B. The classifier should use demographic parity instead of equal opportunity.
- C. Post-processing with per-group thresholds **requires the protected attribute at inference time**, and in **US employment settings the direct use of race at decision time is prohibited even for the purpose of mitigation**. Chapter 04 calls this out as an anti-pattern — the algorithm is auditable and reversible but is not *legally deployable* in this jurisdiction. The Pareto table must carry a **legal-deployability flag** per row so this is caught before the operating point is picked; if the flag rules out post-processing, the sweep must include pre- or in-processing alternatives (Reweighing, Exponentiated Gradient Reduction, Adversarial Debiasing) whose output serving does not require the protected attribute at inference.
- D. The model should be retrained from scratch without the protected attribute.

## 10. A team ships a loan-denial model with a **KernelSHAP** global bar chart as the only explainability artefact. The model is a gradient-boosted tree ensemble, and the sample budget on the KernelSHAP call is 100. The downstream consumer is the adverse-action notice under ECOA / Regulation B. Per chapter 05, what are the two specific things wrong with this setup?

- A. Nothing — SHAP is SHAP regardless of variant or sample budget.
- B. The team should have used LIME instead.
- C. Two problems. First, **KernelSHAP on a tree model is the wrong tool** — TreeSHAP computes exact Shapley values for tree ensembles in closed form, while KernelSHAP is a sampled approximation whose error scales with the sample budget. KernelSHAP at 100 samples on a tree model is slower, noisier, and less faithful than the exact TreeSHAP answer. Second, **a global bar chart is the wrong shape for an adverse-action notice** — ECOA / Regulation B requires the *principal reasons* for the specific decision, which an **Alibi counterfactual explanation** (the minimal rewrite of the applicant's features that would have flipped the decision) answers directly in actionable consumer-facing language. A SHAP local waterfall may support the notice if it identifies specific actionable features; a global feature-importance chart does not. Chapter 05 names both failure modes: "KernelSHAP on a tree model" and "Adverse-action notice built from SHAP bar chart alone."
- D. The team should drop explainability entirely.

## 11. A report presents LIME explanations as the headline and does not include SHAP. A reviewer argues "LIME and SHAP usually agree anyway; running both is duplication." Per chapter 05, what does the chapter say is the specific role of LIME in a mod-106 explainability slice, and what breaks if LIME and SHAP disagreement is suppressed?

- A. LIME is the headline and SHAP is a compare-point.
- B. The reviewer is correct; drop one and keep the other.
- C. Chapter 05 pins **LIME as a compare-point, not a headline**. The engineering purpose of running LIME alongside SHAP is to detect **explanation instability** — when LIME (a locally weighted linear surrogate) and SHAP agree on the top-`k` features for an instance, both are picking up a real local structure and confidence in the local explanation is higher. When they **disagree strongly**, that is a signal that the local explanation is unstable — either SHAP's assumptions do not fit (KernelSHAP under-sampled; wrong variant) or LIME's linear-surrogate assumption is violated by a non-smooth local region. Suppressing the disagreement (picking whichever agrees with the preferred narrative) is post-hoc rationalisation and the chapter names it as an anti-pattern ("explanations disagreeing silently"). The cross-tool agreement rate (and the tail of disagreement cases) is a required section in the slice.
- D. LIME and SHAP always agree, so disagreement is a bug to suppress.

## 12. A vendor delivers a LL144-shape audit report that is signed by a compliance consultant the vendor retains on an ongoing basis. The employer's level-15 governance analyst files the report; the level-25 risk engineer who built the evaluation is named as a co-signer. Per chapter 06, what is specifically wrong with this arrangement, and what does the chapter say is the correct three-way split?

- A. Nothing — the arrangement is standard.
- B. The level-25 risk engineer should sign as the independent auditor because they know the system best.
- C. Two problems. First, **NYC Local Law 144 explicitly requires the auditor be independent** — not involved in using, developing, or distributing the tool — and a compliance consultant materially dependent on the vendor fails that independence bar ("auditor-employed-by-vendor" is the specific chapter-06 anti-pattern). Second, **the level-25 risk engineer is not independent of the system they built** and cannot sign the external audit even as a co-signer; chapter 06 pins the three-way split as *this role (L25) builds the report; `ai-governance-analyst` (L15) files it with narrative wrapping and tracks the annual re-audit clock; an independent third-party auditor (typically a ForHumanity Independent AI Auditor or a BABL AI Algorithm Bias Auditor at an accredited firm) audits the report and signs or rejects*. The three responsibilities do not collapse — LL144 enforces this at the statute level, and the engineering discipline is to produce hashed, pinned, reproducible artefacts the external auditor can cite without re-running the evaluation from scratch.
- D. The level-15 analyst should sign the audit instead of filing it.

## Answer key

1. **C.** Chapter 01, "Motivation" (metric roulette) and "Worked example — the metric-choice five-liner": metric choice is a policy decision pinned *before* authoring the evaluation, anchored to the harm model and the regulator. The five-liner — decision context, protected-attribute partition, primary harm, headline metric, metrics explicitly not headlined — is the shippable shape. The decision-table row for US employment screening names disparate impact / four-fifths as the headline per the EEOC Uniform Guidelines.
2. **B.** Chapter 01, "Chouldechova (2016)" and "Base-rate implication of demographic parity": at unequal base rates, predictive parity and equalised odds cannot both hold — this is the result that ended the ProPublica / Northpointe COMPAS debate as a matter of pure mathematics. The engineering move is to pick a side with policy justification, not to pretend the collision is solvable.
3. **C.** Chapter 02, "The two libraries at a glance" and "Which library for which job — the reach-for table": neither library is a superset of the other, and the shippable pattern uses both — Fairlearn for assessment / reporting via `MetricFrame`, AIF360 for the named-paper mitigation algorithm you need (Reweighing, Adversarial Debiasing, Calibrated Equalised Odds Post-Processing, etc.).
4. **B.** Chapter 02, "Anti-patterns" ("Non-intersectional-only" and "Sample-size blind"): low-power cells are flagged with a Wilson CI and a caveat, not deleted; intersectional slices are required by the slice-report shape and by the LL144 shape in chapter 06. Deleting low-power intersectional cells hits both anti-patterns at once.
5. **C.** Chapter 03, "Individual fairness — Dwork et al. metric-fairness" and "Anti-patterns" ("Individual fairness with a `d` that encodes the bias"): the similarity metric is where the ethical judgement lives; a `d` built from documented proxies for the protected attribute certifies the bias it was supposed to detect. Diagnostics use a feature distance that *excludes* protected attributes and known proxies, and the exclusion list ships in the report.
6. **B.** Chapter 03, "Causal fairness — Kusner et al. counterfactual fairness" (the three preconditions — defensible DAG, identifiability, means to compute) and "Anti-patterns" ("Causal fairness without a defensible DAG"). A whiteboard DAG without literature citations or identifiability checks produces a number that survives no scrutiny; the honest deliverable when the DAG is contested is a "DAG not defensible" note that references the harm-model row needing the diagnostic.
7. **C.** Chapter 03, "Holistic AI's multi-dimensional trustworthiness sweep" and "Anti-patterns" ("Holistic AI sweep shipped as the conclusion"): the sweep's value is *breadth* — surfacing the axis you did not think to look at — and its red flags convert to targeted evaluations. Its output is not the audit conclusion on any axis.
8. **C.** Chapter 04, "The accuracy / fairness trade-off as a curve", "Anti-patterns" ("Sweep without per-group accuracy", "'We picked the middle point'"), and "Fairness / accuracy trade-off — the report structure": the output is the Pareto plot + table + chosen operating point *with* a written justification citing the harm model and appetite band, and per-group accuracy is a first-class column on every row.
9. **C.** Chapter 04, "Post-processing — adjust the decision after the model runs" and "Anti-patterns" ("Post-processing that requires `A` at inference in a setting where `A` cannot be collected"); chapter 06 reinforces the jurisdictional constraint. The legal-deployability flag on the Pareto table catches this; where it rules out post-processing, the sweep must cover pre- / in-processing alternatives whose serving path does not read the protected attribute.
10. **C.** Chapter 05, "SHAP — Shapley-value attributions" (TreeSHAP exact for trees; KernelSHAP approximate model-agnostic), "Alibi — anchor and counterfactual explanations" (counterfactuals are the shape adverse-action notices need under ECOA / GDPR), and "Anti-patterns" ("KernelSHAP on a tree model"; "Adverse-action notice built from SHAP bar chart alone"). Use TreeSHAP for the tree model; ship counterfactual explanations for the adverse-action notice.
11. **C.** Chapter 05, "LIME — the compare-point" and "Anti-patterns" ("Ignoring LIME / SHAP disagreement" and "Explanations disagreeing silently"): LIME's role is to provide a cross-check whose agreement / disagreement with SHAP is a signal about explanation stability. The cross-tool agreement rate and the disagreement tail are required sections in the explainability slice.
12. **C.** Chapter 06, "NYC Local Law 144 — AEDT bias audit" (independence requirement), "Anti-patterns" ("Auditor-employed-by-vendor"), and "The L15 / L25 / independent-auditor split at the report boundary": LL144 enforces independence at the statute level, and the three roles do not collapse — this role *builds* the report with hashed, reproducible artefacts; L15 *files* it; an independent third party (ForHumanity IAA or BABL AI Algorithm Bias Auditor) *audits and signs* it.
