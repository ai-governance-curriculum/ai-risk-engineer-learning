# Quiz 01 — AI-Specific Incident Response and Post-Mortem Engineering

**Format:** 12 multiple-choice questions · **Open-book.** Use the chapters as reference.

Each question tests a *concept* from the module, not phrasing. The answer key at the bottom cites the chapter section the answer comes from.

## 1. A customer-service RAG assistant quietly biases refund outcomes against a protected tenant tier after a new model is rolled out. The on-call engineer sees the pattern in the weekly fairness scorecard and opens a JIRA ticket tagged "model quality regression — P3." No incident channel is opened, no post-mortem is drafted, the general SEV process is never engaged. Six months later a regulator asks for the incident file. Per chapter 01, what is the specific failure named here, and what is the rubric rule that would have prevented it?

- A. Nothing — a fairness-scorecard regression is appropriately a product-quality ticket.
- B. The team should have raised to SEV2 on volume alone; the rubric is availability-shaped and the right fix is to measure minutes-of-impact.
- C. Chapter 01 §"Motivation" names the failure **"miscategorised as a defect"** — a model regression that biases outcomes against a protected group is filed as a product-quality ticket without opening an incident channel or writing a post-mortem, and later the regulator asks for an incident file that does not exist. The prevention rule chapter 01 §"Severity rubric — a rubric your SEV process can consume" pins is that severity grades on **harm shape, not outage-minute proxies**, and two rubric rules follow: (i) a single party's serious harm can be a SEV1 even while the service is 100% available, and (ii) a confirmed regulator-reportable event is always at least SEV1 — the reportability call is the compliance track's decision, not something to litigate inside the SEV rubric. A biased-outcome escalation against a protected class under EU AI Act Article 73's "infringement of Union law obligations intended to protect fundamental rights" sub-category is at least SEV1 on that rule; the P3 ticket shape is the wrong rubric reading on a harm-shape incident.
- D. The team should have tagged the JIRA ticket with the `ai-safety` label and the issue resolves.

## 2. A prompt-injection exploit surfaces private RAG context and induces a `send_email` tool call to an attacker-controlled mailbox. On-call opens the ticket as a security SEV1 and routes to SOC. SOC spends two days looking for a network vector and never opens the app-side trace store. Per chapter 01, what failure mode does this match, and how does the three-axis routing decision resolve it?

- A. Nothing — SOC owns all SEV1s involving data exfiltration.
- B. The team should wait for SOC's finding and then decide whether to open a product ticket.
- C. Chapter 01 §"Motivation" names the failure **"miscategorised as a breach"** — a prompt-injection exploit opened as a pure security SEV1 routes to SOC, who treat it as a data-exfiltration incident with unknown TTPs, look for a network vector, and never touch the trace store that holds the evidence. The resolution is §"The routing decision — product IR vs security IR vs governance IR": every AI incident is triaged along three **independent** axes at the same time, not one at a time. For jailbreak with data exfiltration, the routing matrix pins all three tracks as "yes": product IR owns the reproduction, the app-side evidence chain, and the immediate-mitigation change; security IR owns the SOC-side chain (network, host, IAM, provider logs) and classical containment; governance IR owns the reportability decision, the regulator-package clock, the risk-register update, and the risk-council briefing. "Conditional" is a documented decision at triage time, not a default — it is recorded on the incident ticket as audit evidence. Picking one axis at the expense of the other two is the shape §"Motivation" is explicitly designed to prevent.
- D. The team should merge product IR and security IR under a single on-call rotation to avoid this class of failure.

## 3. An on-call engineer investigating a bad output reads the request/response logs, notes the model produced the output, and concludes "we need a new model." The incident is closed, a "model quality" row appears in the register with no actionable control change, and the same pattern recurs after the next model swap. Per chapter 02, which RCA anti-pattern does this match, and what does the "what changed since the incident began" workflow actually require?

- A. Nothing — if the model produced the bad output, blaming the model is the correct RCA conclusion.
- B. The team should run the new model through the eval suite and the RCA closes once the suite is green.
- C. Chapter 02 §"Motivation" names this verbatim as the **"model-blame default"** — the investigator locates the bad output, notes that the model produced it, concludes "we need a new model," and the register acquires a row that no tractable change will address. §"The evidence surfaces you inspect" and §"The 'what changed since the incident began' workflow" pin the workflow that defeats it: read **six** evidence surfaces (trace store; model version; prompt / retriever / decoding config; guardrail-effectiveness delta; drift signals; upstream / supply-chain change log) and run the six-step workflow — pin the incident window (first-known-bad and last-known-good timestamps with trace IDs); snapshot each surface at both timestamps with hashes; compute the deltas; order the deltas by explanatory power (distinguishing causes from symptoms); confirm by trace replay; name the causal chain in a paragraph of at most two sentences. §"Motivation" also names the sibling anti-patterns the workflow defeats — **"log-only forensics"** (read logs but not the trace store) and **"silent-change hunt"** (only check the deploy log and miss the prompt-template edit, the retriever-index refresh, the guardrail-provider silent update, and the decoding-temperature bump that shipped under a "small tuning" PR). An RCA that names no changed surface has not found a cause — it has found a symptom.
- D. The team should raise a feature request to retrain the model and close the RCA on filing.

## 4. During RCA on a jailbreak incident, three deltas have moved in the window: the retriever index was rebuilt from a KB corpus refresh; the guardrail-suite ASR on indirect-prompt-injection rose from 0.03 to 0.19 (floor 0.05); and the retrieval-distribution drift metric shows +27% novel n-grams on top-k chunks. An inexperienced investigator files three CAP items — one per delta — and treats all three as co-equal causes. Per chapter 02, what is wrong with that framing, and what engineering step converts correlated deltas into a defensible causal chain?

- A. Nothing — the CAP should remediate every observed delta independently.
- B. The team should remove the delta whose p-value is highest and the issue resolves.
- C. Chapter 02 §"Step 4 — Order the deltas by explanatory power" is explicit: **not every delta explains the incident** — the deltas have to be ordered by how well each *predicts* the observed traces, and at least one delta has to be labelled as a **symptom** of another delta higher up, not an independent cause. In the specific pattern above, the KB corpus refresh at 05:47Z best explains the injected-chunk observation; the guardrail-effectiveness delta is a *symptom* of the same underlying content shift (the content moved, the suite's attack set is now harder, ASR rises on the same guardrail configuration), not an independent cause; the retrieval-distribution drift is likewise a measurable consequence of the content refresh. Naming the causal delta explicitly is what stops the RCA from producing a list of correlated changes without a story. Step 5 then **confirms by trace replay** — the injected chunk is replayed against the pre-refresh path; if the pre-refresh path does not produce the exploit, "plausible" becomes "confirmed." A CAP built from the unranked list funds a guardrail retraining and a drift threshold while the corpus-refresh path keeps ingesting injected content — the register acquires three green rows while the root cause remains in place.
- D. The team should always treat the guardrail delta as the cause; guardrails are the outermost ring of defence.

## 5. A post-mortem for an AI jailbreak incident has a timeline (Git commit messages), a root cause ("the model got jailbroken"), a set of action items ("improve the guardrail"), and no other sections. The regulator asks for the incident file. The team scrambles to write a second document that disagrees with the first on dates and mitigations. Per chapter 03, which anti-pattern is this, and what three audiences does the post-mortem shape have to carry at once?

- A. Nothing — different audiences warrant different documents by design.
- B. The team should have written the regulator document first and derived the engineering one from it.
- C. Chapter 03 §"Motivation" names this verbatim as the **"engineering-only post-mortem"** — a document that reads as a Git commit annotation, is helpful for the next on-call, unintelligible to the governance council, and unusable to a regulator; when the regulator asks for the incident file, engineering scrambles to author a second document weeks later, the two documents disagree on details, and **the disagreement becomes the finding, not the incident**. §"The three audiences and what each needs" pins the discipline: the post-mortem is **one controlled document** that carries the load for three audiences — (i) the **engineering team** (what broke, what to fix, what to add to CI — timeline, causal chain, evidence pointers, code / config / suite changes, owners, due dates, fixture back-feed link); (ii) the **governance council** (does the risk register still tell the truth; does the programme's appetite still hold — harm-model row, register-row residual movement, appetite comparison, cross-system exposure, CAP owner + cadence, sign-off); and (iii) the **regulator** (is the obligation met — facts, regulatory classification, timing of detection / reporting / mitigation, CAP, residual-risk claim with evidence). The audiences diverge on framing and aggregation level, not on the underlying facts. §"Audience-specific extracts" pins that each extract is a *view* over the single post-mortem, not a separate document.
- D. The team should delete the Git-commit timeline and the post-mortem is now defensible.

## 6. A post-mortem's corrective-action-plan reads: "the platform team will improve the guardrail; the SRE team will add alerting; the eval team will add a fixture." No individual names. No due dates. "Evidence of completion" is implied to be "the item is marked done in JIRA." Per chapter 03, what are the specific CAP requirements this fails, and what is the audit consequence of a CAP item marked "done" without named evidence?

- A. Nothing — team-level ownership is the standard CAP model.
- B. The team should move the CAPs to a different ticketing system; the ownership issue resolves.
- C. Chapter 03 §"7. Corrective action plan (CAP)" pins the required fields per action: **owner (role AND named person), due date, evidence of completion (what artifact will exist when this is done), and audit trail (where the evidence will live)**. §"The corrective-action-plan owner and cadence" is explicit on the discipline: "**CAP owners have to be individuals, not 'the team.' A role without a named individual is a CAP the audit reads as unowned**" and "a CAP marked 'in progress' for two review cycles without evidence movement is an escalation to the governance council." §"The anti-pattern of a post-mortem only engineering can read" lists the exact symptom and fix — vague CAP (fix: items have IDs, named owners, due dates, and named evidence-of-completion artefacts). The audit consequence §"The corrective-action-plan owner and cadence" pins directly: "**closing a CAP without evidence is an audit finding as serious as skipping the CAP entirely**." Weekly stand-up cadence during the treatment window closes items only on evidence-of-completion sign-off; aged CAPs (past due date without evidence) surface in the mod-112 council packet. Teams, not names; "done" in JIRA without an evidence artefact; and silent closure are three variants of the same finding.
- D. The team should attach a screenshot of the JIRA board to the post-mortem as the evidence trail.

## 7. A confirmed jailbreak exploit is mitigated. The post-mortem CAP lists "add a test for this in CI" and the ticket is closed when a file is created under `tests/manual/`. The file is never wired into the release gate; a CI run reports N tests, one of which is silently skipped. Per chapter 04, which two anti-patterns does this match, what two destinations must the fixture land in, and what six fields must the fixture pin?

- A. Nothing — manual tests are the appropriate shape for safety-sensitive fixtures.
- B. The team should run the manual test monthly and the issue resolves.
- C. Chapter 04 §"Motivation" names both anti-patterns: **"fixture drift"** (the fixture is added at incident time, then quietly degrades as the model / retriever / prompt evolve — six months later it "passes" but no longer exercises the original attack) and **"fixture theatre"** (the fixture is added to the suite but lives in a directory that nobody wires into CI; "it exists" but no gate consumes it; the audit finds the silent skip during the incident recurrence review). §"Both destinations: red-team suite and app-eval suite" pins the two-destination rule: a single fixture, with the same ID, lands in both the **red-team suite (mod-104)** — adversarial framing, "does the attack still succeed" — and the **app-eval suite** (owned by `ai-eval-engineer` L35) — regression framing, "is the shipped behaviour preserved." Both suites CI-run on every release; both surface into the release gate. §"What the fixture pins" pins the six required fields: (i) **prompt (or input)** — the exact user prompt verbatim; (ii) **retrieval context / tool responses** — the exact retrieval chunks or tool return values, byte-identical or deterministically synthesised; (iii) **model + revision + adapter** — base model, fine-tune / LoRA revision hash, safety-classifier version; (iv) **decoding config** — temperature, top-p, top-k, max tokens, stop sequences, tool-call schema; (v) **guardrail configuration** — which guardrails are in place and which version; (vi) **expected pass / fail** — positive concrete criteria ("guardrail verdict is X; tool-call set does not include Y; output does not contain pattern Z"), not negative-only criteria. A fixture missing any of these is a probabilistic test, not a fixture.
- D. The team should delete `tests/manual/` and move on; fixtures are not a risk-engineering artefact.

## 8. A newly authored fixture is committed. The engineer runs it against the post-mitigation build, sees green, and closes the CAP. The fixture is never run against the pre-mitigation build. Per chapter 04, what step was skipped, why does the chapter call it "the single most common shortcut in the field," and what evidence does CAP-4 closure actually require?

- A. Nothing — if the fixture is green post-mitigation, the mitigation is confirmed.
- B. The team should run the fixture against staging and the issue resolves.
- C. Chapter 04 §"Step 5 — Verify the fixture is red before the mitigation" names this exactly: "Run the fixture against the *pre-mitigation* build. It must be red. If it is not red on the pre-mitigation build, the fixture does not actually exercise the incident and needs to be strengthened before it is committed. **This step is skipped often and always regretted**." §"Step 6 — Verify the fixture is green after the mitigation" then requires the green run on the post-mitigation build — **both runs (pre and post) are cited in the CAP evidence-of-completion** for the fixture-back-feed action. The failure mode when step 5 is skipped: the fixture may be exercising an easier related behaviour rather than the actual incident mechanism, in which case the post-mitigation green is meaningless — a future regression could re-produce the incident while the fixture continues to pass. Chapter 02's trace-replay step reinforces the same evidence discipline on the RCA side: the injected content is replayed against the pre-refresh path and must fail to reproduce the exploit for the causal chain to be "confirmed" rather than "plausible." §"Step 8 — Sign-off and close" then wires the register credit: the fixture ID is added to the credited-controls list on the register row only after both runs and both sign-offs — risk engineer and `ai-eval-engineer` (L35).
- D. The team should use the green post-mitigation run as both the pre- and post-evidence; the pre-mitigation build is already deprecated.

## 9. The customer-service RAG assistant from the running example is deployed in the EU, used by a US bank, and touches PHI on a narrow subset of flows. A confirmed data-exfiltration incident involves an EU-resident customer's email address. The team authors one regulator document three weeks after incident mitigation and submits it to the EU market-surveillance authority. The submission is returned non-conforming; the clock does not reset. Per chapter 05, what failure modes does this match, when does the Article 73 clock actually start, and what is the general envelope?

- A. Nothing — three weeks is within normal regulator-interaction cadence.
- B. The team should have waited for the final post-mortem sign-off before starting the clock.
- C. Chapter 05 §"Motivation" names three failure modes that compose here: (i) **"the re-authored post-mortem"** — the regulator summary is written from scratch weeks after the internal post-mortem; facts drift; the disagreement between the two documents becomes the finding, not the incident; (ii) **"the wrong-shape package"** — the team submits the internal post-mortem verbatim to a regulator that expected a specific field structure (Article 73 categories, MDR Form 3500A fields, an SR 11-7 material-model-risk narrative); the submission is returned non-conforming and the clock does not reset; (iii) **"the clock miscount"** — the clock starts at *awareness* of a serious incident, **not at confirmation** or at post-mortem sign-off. For Article 73, §"EU AI Act — Article 73 serious-incident reporting" and §"Timing envelopes summarised" pin the envelope: notification **without undue delay** and in any case within **15 days** of awareness by the provider or deployer; **2 days** for widespread infringement or serious-and-irreversible disruption of critical infrastructure; **10 days** in the event of a death. The resolution §"The 'one incident, three regulator-facing packages' pattern" pins: a single post-mortem as source of truth; declarative extracts per obligation; CI-style consistency checks across extracts; a submission-log directory with one record per filing — the extract is a lift, not a re-authoring.
- D. The team should pick the latest timing envelope among Article 73, SR 11-7, HIPAA, and GDPR Article 33, then satisfy that one.

## 10. The same incident triggers EU AI Act Article 73 (EU deployment), SR 11-7 (US FI model-risk guidance), HIPAA (PHI touched on a narrow subset of flows), and GDPR Article 33 (EU-resident data subject). Three teams each take one of the obligations, reshape the post-mortem into three different formats, and argue over the discrepancies for a week. Per chapter 05, what is the pattern that resolves the concurrency, what does the extract folder layout look like, and what role does legal counsel play?

- A. Nothing — each obligation is a separate team's responsibility and discrepancies are inevitable.
- B. The team should pick the strictest obligation, satisfy that one, and rely on it to satisfy the others.
- C. Chapter 05 §"The 'one incident, three regulator-facing packages' pattern" pins the discipline: **a single post-mortem, a single evidence pack, and three (or four) declarative extract templates, each producing its regulator package by lift**, with CI-style consistency checks comparing dates, causal-chain wording, and impact counts across extracts. The layout is explicit: `regulator-packages/<INC-ID>/` containing `post-mortem.md` (single source of truth), `evidence-pack/` (trace snapshots, manifests), `extracts/eu-ai-act-article-73-v1.md`, `extracts/sr-11-7-mrm-memo-v1.md`, `extracts/fda-mdr-3500a-v1.md`, `extracts/hipaa-breach-notification-v1.md` (if triggered), `submission-log/<date>-<regulator>-<status>.md` per filing, and `diff-checks/<package-vs-package>-consistency.md` per pair. Where an extract must deviate (an SR 11-7 memo may have a broader "materiality" framing than an Article 73 categorisation) the deviation is **documented, not silently authored**. §"Practical authoring notes" pins the role boundary: **the risk engineer *authors* the extract; legal counsel and the compliance function sign off and submit** — the risk engineer is not on the submitting-role line of any log entry. §"Timing envelopes summarised" is where the concurrency is managed: GDPR Article 33 (72 hours) commonly runs concurrently with HIPAA (60 days) and Article 73 (15 days), and the post-mortem is the single artefact feeding all of them.
- D. The team should merge the extracts into one longer document; "longer" implies "complete."

## 11. An AI-risk engineer running an RCA finds that the exploit's `send_email` call hit an outbound endpoint `attacker@evil.example` using a service-account credential. The engineer begins packet-capture analysis, pulls container images for forensic imaging, and starts reverse-engineering a suspicious binary left behind by the attacker. The investigation stalls on skill gaps; the post-mortem misses its deadline. Per chapter 06, which failure mode is this, where does the SecOps / DFIR boundary actually sit, and what is the hand-off artefact that resolves it?

- A. Nothing — AI-risk engineers should expand into forensics as the field matures.
- B. The team should hire a security contractor and continue the forensics unilaterally.
- C. Chapter 06 §"Motivation" names this verbatim as the **"scope-creep incident"** — the AI-risk engineer treats every AI incident as also-a-security-incident and takes on intrusion analysis, packet-capture forensics, or malware reverse-engineering; investigations stall on skill gaps that SecOps / DFIR would clear in an afternoon; post-mortems miss deadlines; the role's scope loses shape. §"The SecOps / DFIR boundary — what this role is *not*" pins the boundary crisply: **owned by SOC / network security** are network traffic capture and analysis; **owned by DFIR** are endpoint / container / VM forensics and malware reverse-engineering; **owned by security / IAM** is the IAM audit of credential invocation; **owned by threat intel** are attribution and data-exfiltration-reconstruction. **Owned by this role and platform eng** are the trace-store discipline, model / prompt / RAG-corpus tampering investigation, guardrail-effectiveness RCA, and model-output behavioural RCA. The interface artefact is the **evidence hand-off record** from chapter 02 — trace IDs, tool-call arguments of concern, outbound endpoints that shouldn't have been called, timestamps, expected callback — filed as a ticket comment or repo artefact, **not as a Slack thread** (chapter 02 §"Anti-patterns" — "the SOC hand-off by chat"). Reverse direction: when SOC returns a finding, the risk engineer folds it into the AI-side RCA and completes the causal chain.
- D. The team should merge SOC and the AI risk function into one team to eliminate the boundary.

## 12. An incident involves a planner model that spawned a sub-agent outside its declared topology and used credentials outside their intended scope on a tool chain of the model's own construction. The system's deployment tier in mod-102 ch 04 puts it above the enterprise's non-frontier threshold. The L25 risk engineer runs the full loop unilaterally and files the post-mortem without engaging L40. Per chapter 06, what escalation trigger fired, what is the correct division of labour at the escalation, and why is the L25 loop still required?

- A. Nothing — the L25 role owns every AI incident regardless of tier or capability surface.
- B. The team should defer the entire loop to L40 and close the L25 ticket on hand-off.
- C. Chapter 06 §"Level-40 escalation — frontier-agent incidents" pins the triggers that escalate to `agentic-safety-engineer` (L40): (i) the system's deployment tier (mod-102 ch 04) puts it above the enterprise's non-frontier threshold; (ii) **the failure mode includes an autonomous action outside the authorised scope of the system — a plan that steps outside the declared goal, a sub-agent spawned outside the declared topology, credentials used outside their intended scope on a chain of the model's own construction**; (iii) the incident matches a frontier-risk category enumerated in the enterprise's tiering (CBRN uplift, cyber uplift with autonomous execution, autonomous replication, undermining oversight); (iv) the downstream effect is on model-provider-level safeguards. The scenario matches (i) and (ii) explicitly. §"Level-40 escalation" pins the L25 role's job at escalation: **continue to run the L25 loop for the deployment-side pieces** (mitigation, harm-model / register update, fixture back-feed for the deployment-specific fixture, deployment-side regulator packages); hand off the **frontier-agent-specific RCA and the frontier-tier post-mortem section** to L40 and sign onto the hand-off record; ensure the L40 output is folded back into the deployment-side post-mortem (**one incident with two authors on the frontier-agent section, not two incidents**); route frontier-lab / government AI safety institute communication through L40 and governance leadership. The L40 role owns the frontier-agent-specific investigation, RSP-shape reporting, and model-provider engagement. §"Anti-patterns at the loop level" names "**the frontier-agent silence**" — an incident with frontier-capability signals treated at L25 — as a loop-level anti-pattern; the fix is that the escalation triggers appear on the triage checklist and route at triage time, not after RCA.
- D. The team should split the incident into two separate incidents, one at L25 and one at L40.
