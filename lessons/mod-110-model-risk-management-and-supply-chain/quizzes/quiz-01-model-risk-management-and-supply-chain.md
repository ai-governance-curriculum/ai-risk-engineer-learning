# Quiz 01 — Model Risk Management (MRM) for ML/AI and AI Supply Chain

**Format:** 12 multiple-choice questions · **Open-book.** Use the chapters as reference.

Each question tests a *concept* from the module, not phrasing. The answer key at the bottom cites the chapter the answer comes from.

---

## 1. An ML team at a US bank ships a Hugging Face model card, a fine-tune script, and a notebook of evaluation plots to the Model Risk Management (MRM) function. The validator reads the submission and blocks the model as "un-validatable." The team argues they ran more evaluations than any previous model in the programme and the quality is higher than the credit-scorecard the validator just cleared. Per chapter 01, what is the specific failure and how does the SR 11-7 shape resolve it?

- A. Nothing — Hugging Face model cards are the industry standard; the validator is applying an outdated process.
- B. The team should run additional evaluations until the validator stops asking.
- C. Chapter 01 §"Motivation" names the failure: it is a **packaging failure, not an engineering failure**. The validator's document taxonomy has slots for *conceptual soundness*, *outcome analysis*, *ongoing monitoring*, *implementation testing* and so on — the SR 11-7 / OCC 2011-12 MRM packet shape — and the submitted artefacts are organised by *tool* (model card, fine-tune script, notebook plots) rather than by MRM heading. Chapter 01 §"MRM-shaped validation documentation for an ML/AI model" pins the resolution: the packet is a folder with a stable table of contents (model description, tiering, data, methodology / conceptual soundness, assumptions, development testing, independent validation, implementation testing, ongoing monitoring plan, change management, third-party components, governance sign-off), and the engineering fix is to *name the MRM heading each piece of evidence files under*. The guidance is deliberately silent on technique but prescriptive on shape — "the same shape survives from linear regression to gradient boosting to a fine-tuned LLM" — so the packaging is the thing that has to change, not the evaluation depth. An MRM reviewer will accept a packet with the right shape even if the underlying evidence is a bit thin, because the shape gives them somewhere to write their findings.
- D. The validator should learn Hugging Face conventions; the team's shape is correct.

## 2. In an MRM programme the same engineer writes the red-team suite, picks the thresholds, runs the evaluations on their own CI, and signs the "independent validation" memo. The team argues this is efficient and that no one else has the context to validate the system. Per chapter 01, which SR 11-7 pillar does this pattern break, and what are the two engineering consequences the chapter pins for AI?

- A. Nothing — if the engineer is senior enough, consolidating validation and development is acceptable.
- B. The team should rename the memo "self-attestation" and the problem resolves.
- C. Chapter 01 §"Independence of validation from development" names the pillar directly — **independence of validation from development is the first SR 11-7 pillar**. The chapter is explicit on the two engineering consequences for AI: (i) **evaluation code is owned by validation, not development** — the mod-104 red-team suite, mod-105 adversarial suite, mod-106 fairness audit, and mod-107 privacy evaluation have their *suite definitions* (which tests run, which thresholds pass, which datasets are used) owned by validation; development can contribute tests and reproduce runs but does not change the pass rule; (ii) **evidence artefacts are signed by validation** — a red-team report in the release evidence packet carries the validator's signature, not the developer's, which in practice means the CI system that runs the suite runs under a validation-owned service account and writes to a validation-controlled artefact store. §"Effective challenge" adds the cultural evidence of independence: the red-team suite must include tests the developer did not write, the register must include rows the developer would not have volunteered, and post-mortems must have a "what did we miss" section owned by someone other than the developer. Independence is a practice, not a document titled "Independence Attestation."
- D. The pillar is only applicable to classical models; AI validation is permitted to collapse the roles.

## 3. A team buys a foundation-model API and hands the vendor the standard SaaS due-diligence questionnaire (SOC 2, availability SLAs, data-processing addendum). They declare the vendor's own uptime dashboard sufficient monitoring. Three weeks later the vendor silently rotates the underlying model; the team's safety evaluations invalidate and a customer complaint surfaces the drift. Per chapter 02, what are the two specific SR 23-4 failures, and what does "monitor on your own signal" actually mean?

- A. Nothing — SOC 2 plus the vendor dashboard is sufficient per industry practice.
- B. The team should switch to the vendor's premium tier and the problem resolves.
- C. Chapter 02 §"Motivation" names both failure modes verbatim: (i) **copy-paste SaaS due diligence** — nothing in the standard questionnaire asks about model-change notification, training-data provenance, adversarial-robustness posture, or safety-classifier behaviour; and (ii) **vendor status page as monitoring** — the vendor's dashboard measures *the vendor's* metrics (availability, latency), not your safety metrics, calibration, or fairness slices. §"Due diligence and third-party selection" pins the AI-specific extension — model identity and version, change notification, training-data provenance, safety-classifier posture, red-team disclosure, adversarial-robustness posture, fairness posture, privacy, incident disclosure, right-to-audit, sub-processors, termination / portability. §"Ongoing monitoring" is explicit on *your-signal* monitoring: a fixed prompt / input set run against the vendor on a cadence with outputs hashed and compared to baseline; safety metrics measured on a rolling sample of production traffic; fairness monitor on the vendor's outputs across *your* demographic slices; cost / usage anomaly detection as a proxy for silent model swap; pinned red-team fixture regression running against the vendor's current version; and vendor-side model card / system card version tracking. "The point of monitoring on your own signal is *catching silent change*. Every foundation-model vendor has, at some point, rotated an underlying model on a stable API tag." The vendor's status page is a derived view of *their* metrics, not evidence of *your* model risk.
- D. SR 23-4 does not apply to AI vendors; it is only about banking service providers.

## 4. A vendor refuses to sign a version-pinning clause and a safety-metric SLA during contract negotiation. The engineer argues the team should walk away from the vendor because the clauses are unsigned. Per chapter 02, what are the three permitted responses to a walked-away clause, and where in the risk-engineering stack does the decision actually sit?

- A. Nothing — any walked-away clause means the vendor must be rejected.
- B. The team should silently accept the gap and continue.
- C. Chapter 02 §"Contract negotiation" is explicit: "For each clause the vendor refuses, you have three options: *walk*, *escalate to the risk council as a residual-risk acceptance*, or *engineer a compensating control on your side* (a monitor, an internal safety classifier, a version-freeze wrapper). The choice is a risk-council decision (mod-112), not an engineer's decision." The compensating-control path is what the "own-signal" monitoring section already builds the toolkit for — a safety monitor on the vendor's output, a drift fixture, a behaviour-hash baseline — but the *risk-register consequence* (residual-risk elevation on the mod-103 rows the vendor participates in) lives on the register, and the decision to tolerate that elevation lives at mod-112. §"Due diligence and third-party selection" adds: "Where the vendor cannot or will not answer a question, note it as a *disclosure gap*. Disclosure gaps do not automatically kill a relationship — they raise the residual risk on the mod-103 rows the vendor participates in, and they require *compensating monitoring* from your side." A walked-away clause without a compensating control and without a register update is the shape that fails under an examiner.
- D. The decision belongs entirely to the engineer at the integration layer.

## 5. A SaMD team drafts a Predetermined Change Control Plan that authorises "ongoing improvements to the model, including retraining on new data as it becomes available." They send it to their FDA regulatory-affairs contact who returns it with a rejection. Per chapter 03, what is the specific failure mode in the PCCP's Description of Modifications, and what are the four elements each modification type must actually specify?

- A. Nothing — FDA's own guidance encourages adaptive wording.
- B. The team should remove the "retraining" word and resubmit.
- C. Chapter 03 §"Description of Modifications" is emphatic: broad wording such as "improvements to the model" is **not a description of modifications — it is a request for unlimited change and it will not clear review**. The chapter and exercise 03 pin the four elements each modification type must specify: (i) **the class of change** (re-training on additional data, threshold adjustment, pre-processing update, hyperparameter re-search within bounds, etc.); (ii) **the scope** — which components are affected, which are held fixed, the *numeric bounds* of the envelope (data-size range, threshold range, hyperparameter search bounds); (iii) **the triggering condition** — what causes the modification to be applied (calibration drift beyond a stated threshold, new input-device compatibility, scheduled quarterly retraining); and (iv) **the out-of-envelope condition** — what forces a new submission instead of an in-envelope change. A worked in-envelope example from §"Description of Modifications": "Re-training on additional data drawn from named sites and patient populations, within a stated size envelope, with the model architecture and inference pipeline held fixed." The modification protocol then specifies *how* each in-envelope change will be verified, validated, and monitored — the data-management plan, re-training plan, performance-evaluation plan (with acceptance criteria and stratification per mod-106), update procedures, cybersecurity assessment (chapters 05-06), and post-modification monitoring (mod-109). The impact assessment then argues, per modification, which risks elevate, which controls contain them, and how acceptance criteria keep residual inside the envelope the original submission established.
- D. The PCCP is a paperwork exercise and the wording does not matter in practice.

## 6. A device team ships a model that updates its weights based on deployment data (reinforcement learning from clinician feedback on misclassifications). The team says the model is "self-improving" and does not need a PCCP because the updates are internal. Per chapter 03, what is the locked-vs-adaptive distinction, and what is the regulatory consequence of this team's posture?

- A. Nothing — adaptive models are permitted as long as the team documents the updates internally.
- B. The team should rename the updates "bug fixes" and the issue resolves.
- C. Chapter 03 §"Locked vs adaptive" is explicit. A **locked** algorithm behaves identically every time it is given the same input; it does not learn from deployment data; updates happen only through explicit re-training that is either inside the PCCP envelope or the subject of a new submission. An **adaptive** algorithm changes its behaviour based on deployment data — continuous online learning, session-level fine-tuning, and reinforcement-learning-from-clinical-feedback are all adaptive. The regulatory consequence: "The FDA has been comfortable with locked algorithms since the earliest AI/ML authorisations. Adaptive algorithms require a PCCP that specifies exactly *how* they adapt, *what* they can adapt to, and *what* stops them adapting outside the envelope. **Adaptive without a PCCP is not permitted in a marketed device**." The engineering consequence the chapter spells out: "if your device is adaptive, the adaptation path itself is a control that must be evidenced. The training-data ingestion pipeline is a controlled surface; the retraining trigger is a controlled surface; the newly trained model must pass the modification-protocol gate before it can replace the incumbent. Every step in that adaptation loop is an artefact." A team shipping RLHF on clinical feedback with no PCCP is operating a marketed adaptive device without a lawful mechanism.
- D. Adaptive is a marketing term; all FDA-cleared devices are implicitly locked.

## 7. An auditor asks a team "for this deployed model, show me every input that went into it and every transformation it went through." The team says they can find it — give them a week — the data is in Slack, a training-script comment, a departed engineer's laptop, and an S3 bucket with no naming convention. Per chapter 04, what does the chapter call this and what is the five-layer provenance stack the auditor is actually asking for?

- A. Nothing — reconstruction over a week is standard practice for provenance questions.
- B. The team should move the Slack messages to a Google Doc and the issue resolves.
- C. Chapter 04 §"Motivation" names this failure directly: **"That is not provenance. That is *reconstruction*."** The engineering fix the chapter pins: "turn provenance into *files with schemas* that live alongside the artefacts they describe, and turn assembly of those files into *build steps that fail if inputs are missing*, not a checklist that fills itself." §"The provenance stack in one diagram (as prose)" names the five composing layers: (i) **data provenance** — Datasheet for Datasets or Data Card per corpus that entered training, fine-tuning, evaluation, retrieval, or prompt context; (ii) **model provenance** — Hugging Face-style Model Card per model artefact (base, fine-tune, safety classifier), linking to the datasets in layer 1; (iii) **component inventory** — an ML-BOM (CycloneDX) listing every ML-specific component the deployed system depends on, extending the software SBOM shape; (iv) **cross-domain SBOM record** — an SPDX 3.0 document using the AI profile, expressing AI-specific relationships alongside the ordinary software SBOM in a single record; (v) **build-integrity attestation** — a SLSA provenance attestation describing how the deployed artefact was built, by which builder, from which sources, at what level of assurance. Chapters 05 and 06 add layer 6 (signing the artefacts and the provenance documents themselves) and layer 7 (scanning the artefacts for malicious content before ingestion). The five layers compose — ML-BOM references Data Cards and Model Cards; SPDX 3.0 can consume the ML-BOM or express the same relationships natively; SLSA attests that layers 1-4 were produced under a controlled build.
- D. Only the deployed weights need provenance; everything else can be reconstructed.

## 8. A team ships an ML-BOM in CycloneDX and is asked by an enterprise procurement contact whether they have an SPDX 3.0 AI-profile record as well. The engineer argues that CycloneDX and SPDX are "competing standards" and that producing both is duplicate work. Per chapter 04, what is the relationship between the two formats, what does the SPDX 3.0 AI profile add that CycloneDX ML-BOM alone does not, and who typically asks for which?

- A. Nothing — CycloneDX and SPDX are interchangeable; pick one and stop.
- B. The team should drop CycloneDX and move exclusively to SPDX.
- C. Chapter 04 §"Layer 4 — SPDX 3.0 with the AI profile" is explicit on the relationship: "Where ML-BOM (CycloneDX) is a lightweight ML-focused inventory, SPDX 3.0 with the AI profile is a broader interchange format that expresses ML relationships in the same document as the software components, licences, and build steps. In practice a mature programme produces *both* — CycloneDX for internal tooling and downstream security-scanning integrations, SPDX 3.0 for external interchange with regulators, standards-body submissions, and enterprise customers who insist on SPDX." The SPDX 3.0 AI profile adds first-class `AIPackage` and `Dataset` package types with `trainedOn`, `hasEvaluation`, and `derivedFrom` relationships, plus licence expressions per package in SPDX's licence-expression syntax — the shape regulators and enterprise procurement lean on. The chapter's consumer heuristic: **"Regulators and enterprise procurement lean SPDX. Security tooling ecosystems lean CycloneDX."** Both can be produced from a single provenance data model in the registry, so the "duplicate work" framing is wrong — one source of truth emits two formats for two audiences. The chapter also pins the SPDX 3.0 publication date (April 2024) as the version that introduced the AI profile; CycloneDX 1.5 (2023) added ML component types, refined in 1.6.
- D. SPDX 3.0 does not have an AI profile; the engineer is correct that producing both is duplicate work.

## 9. A team's inference workers fetch model weights from a URL and load whatever bytes come back without signature verification. The CI pipeline signs on publish but inference never verifies. The team argues that the publish-side signature is sufficient and that adding a load-side check is defence in depth they do not need. Per chapter 05, which specific anti-pattern does this match, what are the four properties the correct check must have, and why does publish-side-only fail?

- A. Nothing — publish-side signing is sufficient; inference-side verification is redundant.
- B. The team should also require humans to sign weights manually.
- C. Chapter 05 §"The 'unsigned weights loaded in prod' anti-pattern" names this verbatim as one of the five collapse-to-anti-pattern defaults: **"The signature is required for publication but not for consumption — inference workers do not verify, only humans do (in a dashboard nobody reads)."** The related anti-patterns in the same section — loader fetches a URL and loads bytes without verification; registry publishes without requiring signature; verifier trusts *any* Sigstore-issued certificate rather than the expected identity; long-lived signing key shared across teams — all collapse to the same default: unsigned (or unverified) weights are permitted to load. §"The runtime story" plus §"The registry-ingestion gate" pin the four properties the correct check must enforce: (a) verify the cosign signature, (b) verify the signing identity is on the **specific-identity allow-list** (any Sigstore cert must not pass), (c) verify the Rekor entry exists and is recent, and (d) **refuse to load** on any of the above failing. The failure mode that publish-side-only cannot catch is the silent swap (attacker with registry write access, or insider under time pressure, replaces the weights file with a look-alike); without inference-side verification the swap is invisible. Chapter 05's fix: "a consistent inference-side pre-load check" — a wrapper written once and applied everywhere where the model-serving stack does not bundle it. Fail-closed is the correct default; fail-open ("we could not verify but let it through") is the same as "no gate at all."
- D. Chapter 05 permits the publish-only signing posture for mature teams.

## 10. A new signing pipeline ships. The engineer sets the cosign gate to accept **any** Sigstore-issued certificate (reasoning: "all Sigstore certificates are trustworthy") and uses a long-lived signing key stored in a shared secret manager that four teams can read. Per chapter 05, which two anti-patterns does this match, what is the correct signing-identity model, and what role does Rekor play in making the identity decision auditable?

- A. Nothing — Sigstore certificates are trustworthy by design.
- B. The team should rotate the long-lived key weekly and the problem resolves.
- C. Chapter 05 §"The 'unsigned weights loaded in prod' anti-pattern" names both failures in its anti-pattern list: (i) **"the signature is verified but the verifier trusts *any* signature, not signatures from the expected identity. An attacker with any Sigstore-issued certificate can sign and pass"** — this is the identity-allow-list failure; and (ii) **"the signature-verification key is a long-lived key stored in a secret manager, and it has been shared across teams for years"** — this is the long-lived-key failure that keyless signing was specifically designed to eliminate. §"Sigstore in six sentences" and §"What you sign, at minimum" pin the correct model: the signer is the **pipeline**, not a human — "the pipeline's OIDC identity (its service account, its workload identity in a cloud IAM system) proves who signed; Rekor makes the signing event auditable. A human should almost never be the direct signer of a production model artefact; when they are, it should be a very deliberate exception with its own audit trail." The flow: cosign generates a short-lived key pair, obtains a signing certificate from **Fulcio** by proving OIDC identity (e.g., the CI job's OIDC token), signs with the ephemeral private key, records the signature in **Rekor** (the Sigstore append-only transparency log), and destroys the private key. Rekor's role is twofold per §"The Rekor transparency log": **non-repudiation** (the signer cannot later claim not to have signed) and **non-falsifiability of the signing timeline** (append-only, witnessed checkpoints, so antedated signatures are detectable). Verification retrieves the certificate and the Rekor entry and confirms the artefact was signed *by the claimed identity* — the identity-allow-list check is the policy that enforces "the claimed identity must be on this list."
- D. Identity allow-lists are optional; the Rekor log is enough on its own.

## 11. A team receives a fine-tuned checkpoint from a public hub and loads it with `torch.load` inside a Python process with production credentials. A senior engineer asks whether the ingestion gate checked the file. The team says they trusted the upload because the file was "a safetensors-style PyTorch model" and besides, the format is standard. Per chapter 06, what is wrong with that reasoning, how does the pickle-and-format threat surface actually work, and what is the correct scanner wiring?

- A. Nothing — PyTorch checkpoints are safe by default.
- B. The team should run `torch.load` inside a Docker container and the issue resolves.
- C. Chapter 06 §"Motivation" names this failure verbatim: **"Pickle-based checkpoint from Hugging Face loads code on `torch.load`. A team fetches a fine-tune from a public hub, calls `torch.load` in a Python process with production credentials, and the pickle's `__reduce__` executes an attacker's payload — exfiltrating environment variables, planting a persistent shell, or writing a backdoor into the model weights before the process even prints a log line."** §"The pickle-and-format threat surface" pins the format table: pickle (`.pkl`, `.pickle`, `.pt`, `.pth`, `.bin` produced by `torch.save`), joblib, and `.npy`/`.npz` with `allow_pickle=True` are **high-risk** because the Python `pickle` module is Turing-complete and executes `__reduce__` on load; TensorFlow SavedModel (`tf.py_function` escapes), Keras H5 (Lambda layers), and ONNX with custom operators are **medium-to-high-risk** — "format-swap does not eliminate the code-execution surface — it only changes which scanner needs to catch it"; **safetensors** and **GGUF** are safe-by-design (header + tensor buffers, no code) and should be preferred for weights whenever the framework supports them. The correct scanner wiring is the chapter's two-tool baseline: **Protect AI ModelScan** (cross-format — PyTorch pickle, TensorFlow SavedModel, Keras H5, others — reports findings with severity levels) *and* **Hugging Face picklescan** (static analysis of pickle streams, enumerates classes and callables the pickle would invoke, allow-list of safe deserialisers) — run both; ModelScan for breadth, picklescan for pickle-specific depth. The ingestion-gate policy runs these **before** the signature checks per §"The ingestion-gate policy": "cheap and destructive checks first — the gate should fail *fast* on cheap checks so that operators do not wait five minutes for a signature check to time out on an artefact that was going to be rejected on format." The commercial layer (HiddenLayer AISec, Protect AI Guardian) sits above the open-source baseline and is an L35 security-platform decision.
- D. Pickle risk has been mitigated by the Python 3 pickle module and no scanner is needed.

## 12. A team's registry has accumulated 40 open scanner exceptions over six months — each granted once, none revisited. A reviewer flags the backlog. The team argues the exceptions are "already approved" and that re-visiting them is wasted work. Per chapter 06, what is the specific signal a growing exception backlog carries, what are the five steps of the correct exception process, and where does the decision to grant or extend a waiver actually sit?

- A. Nothing — once an exception is approved it stands indefinitely.
- B. The team should archive the exceptions and the backlog resolves.
- C. Chapter 06 §"The exception path" is explicit that exceptions are a controlled path, **not a default bypass**, with five steps: (i) **written request from the owner** naming the check that would fail, why the failure is acceptable, and the compensating control; (ii) **risk-council decision (mod-112)** with a **time-bound waiver** — waivers are dated and expire; (iii) **register update** recording the residual-risk elevation and the compensating control; (iv) **monitor confirming the compensating control is present and effective** — the monitor fires the incident-response playbook if the compensating control degrades; (v) **waiver review at expiration** — either extended (with fresh justification) or the artefact must clear the gate cleanly by the deadline. The chapter is explicit on the backlog signal: **"An organisation whose registry has a large exception backlog is one where the gate is misconfigured — either the thresholds are too tight for reality, the exception process is too easy, or the underlying artefact quality is too low. All three are addressable; ignoring them is not."** §"Where this module hands off — the level ladder" and the exercise-06 acceptance criteria pin the ownership: granting / extending waivers is a **risk-council decision at mod-112** — not an engineer's decision, not an L35 platform-team decision. The engineer's role at L25 is to *encode* the council's thresholds, identity allow-lists, and granted exceptions in the gate file, surface the backlog to the council on cadence, and raise the misconfiguration signal when the backlog trends up. Exercise 06's stretch "exception-backlog audit" pins the output format: count by age and severity; a trending-up count is gate-misconfiguration evidence.
- D. The chapter permits permanent exceptions once the first review is clean.
