# Quiz 01 — Continuous Monitoring, Post-Market Surveillance, and Risk-Register Integration

**Format:** 12 multiple-choice questions · **Open-book.** Use the chapters as reference.

Each question tests a *concept* from the module, not phrasing. The answer key at the bottom cites the chapter the answer comes from.

---

## 1. A team points at a Grafana panel of model accuracy over time and credits it as the monitor on a register row for `HR-CS-QUAL-03`. The panel has no alert rule and no owner; the on-call occasionally screenshots it into a Slack thread when a stakeholder asks. A reviewer blocks the row from being credited. Per chapter 01, which five conditions must the artifact satisfy to count as a monitor, and why does a dashboard fail?

- A. Nothing — a chart maintained on a cadence counts as a monitor.
- B. The team should add a second chart for comparison.
- C. Chapter 01 §"Why an ad-hoc dashboard does not count as a monitor" names five conditions, all of which must hold: (i) **a defined signal** — the number has a name, a unit, and a semantics sentence; (ii) **a threshold** — a rule for when the signal is a signal, not a chart the eye interprets; (iii) **a cadence** — a batch schedule or a streaming window; the monitor runs whether or not anyone is looking; (iv) **a routing** — when the threshold is tripped, the alert reaches a named on-call by a named channel *and* the register row is updated deterministically; (v) **an evidence trail** — every tripped signal is filed with a timestamp, the raw value, and the context needed for post-hoc review. A Grafana panel satisfies at most (i); without a threshold, a cadence, a deterministic register-side effect, and a filed tripped-signal record, it is a dashboard. Dashboards are useful for exploration but do not carry regulatory or register load; they are not credited on register rows.
- D. The five conditions apply to safety-monitor signals only; drift-and-quality dashboards are exempt.

## 2. A team drafts a signal contract row where `signal_source_owner` is "the MLOps team" and `deferral_boundary` is blank. The pipeline that emits the metric is owned by `ai-infra-mlops` (peer L25) but the row does not cite the peer boundary. The row's thresholds and register effect are otherwise fully populated. Per chapter 01, what is the specific defect, and what is the schema's rule about `deferral_boundary`?

- A. Nothing — if `signal_source_owner` names a team, the boundary is implied.
- B. The row should drop the `signal_source_owner` field and roll it into a free-text note.
- C. Chapter 01 §"What a signal contract is" lists `deferral_boundary` as one of the fifteen required columns, and §"What the contract is *not*" and §"Interfaces with adjacent modules" pin that the peer boundaries to `ai-infra-mlops` (L25) on the pipeline and `ai-eval-engineer` (L30) on the app-eval online loop are the two the audit trail most commonly muddles. The schema rule is explicit: **"Rows without every column filled are *drafts*. A draft contract is not shippable."** The `deferral_boundary` column has to name the peer track *and* where the boundary sits — the pipeline itself is not re-owned here, but the fact that it is owned elsewhere is on the row. Writing the boundary down is the chapter-06 discipline that "stops the ownership fog"; omitting it produces exactly the under-scoping failure mode chapter 06 §"Motivation" names.
- D. `deferral_boundary` is optional when the monitor is internally owned.

## 3. A team's drift-monitoring config runs a per-feature KS test on each of the top-20 input features daily. The population-level metrics stay quiet for three weeks while per-slice quality on a protected-attribute slice regresses by 12%. A reviewer flags the shape as blind. Per chapter 02, what two disciplines are missing, and which register row does the slice signal affect?

- A. Nothing — population-level KS is the industry-standard drift check.
- B. The team should switch to PSI on the same features.
- C. Chapter 02 pins two disciplines. First, **metric choice has to match the shape of the shift** — §"Types of drift" names per-feature KS as sensitive to univariate marginal shifts but explicitly notes that "a univariate KS on each feature will miss a joint shift where every marginal looks stable," and separately that aggregate metrics smooth per-slice drift away. Second, **per-slice monitoring is a distinct signal family** — §"Per-slice monitoring" is explicit: "the `signal_semantics` names the slice definition explicitly," "the threshold is per-slice — a per-slice trip counts even if the aggregate is quiet," and "the register effect points at the fairness-tagged row, not the drift-tagged row, if the affected slice is a protected group." The per-slice signal routes to the mod-106 fairness-tagged register row with the fairness liaison on the routing; skipping per-slice monitoring is one of the most common findings a fairness auditor names.
- D. Per-slice monitoring belongs to `ai-eval-engineer` at L30; the chapter-02 row set is population-only.

## 4. A team has to pick a detector for (a) tabular feature-profile drift over a reference window, (b) a weekly batch quality regression against a golden set with a pass/fail test suite, and (c) an online change-point detection on streaming token-level scores. They propose using one tool (whichever they install first) for all three. Per chapter 02's working-toolset rule of thumb, what is the correct per-signal choice, and why does "one tool for all three" fail?

- A. Nothing — any of whylogs, Evidently, or Alibi Detect covers all three workloads equivalently.
- B. The team should use WhyLabs commercial for all three signals.
- C. Chapter 02 §"The working toolset" names three open-source projects, each closing a different workload. (a) **Tabular feature-profile drift** — reach for **whylogs**: it emits per-batch profiles (statistical summaries), the profile is the reference-window artifact, and drift metrics (KS-like, PSI, mean/quantile) are computed on the profile pair; cheap to store, mergeable across batches. (b) **Batch quality regression with pass/fail** — reach for **Evidently AI**: reports run on the row's cadence, the test suite is the threshold vehicle, and a failed test-suite emits an event that updates the register. (c) **Online change-point / streaming detectors** — reach for **Alibi Detect**: it implements online detectors (page-Hinkley, CUSUM), MMD on multivariate embeddings, and the broader statistical detector set whylogs and Evidently do not focus on. The chapter's explicit rule of thumb table pairs each signal shape to the tool that fits; "one tool for all three" ships a mismatched detector for at least two workloads. The chapter is clear that "you will likely run all three. That is fine; each closes a different set of rows in the contract."
- D. The three tools are interchangeable; the choice is cosmetic.

## 5. A team's prompt-injection detector alarms on any rate increase above the rolling baseline. During a model rollout, the firing rate *drops* to near-zero while total request volume stays steady; the team's alerting stays silent and the register records nothing. Two weeks later a post-mortem finds the classifier HTTP endpoint had been failing open the whole time. Per chapter 03, which specific rule did the alerting miss, and what register-side event should a "rate down while volume steady" case produce?

- A. Nothing — a drop in firing rate is a good sign; no alert is needed.
- B. The team should only alarm on rate increases greater than 5x baseline.
- C. Chapter 03 §"Deviation is a two-sided signal" is explicit: guardrail firing rate is a **two-sided** deviation signal, not a one-sided "rate up" signal. The "rate down while overall volume steady" branch is specifically named as the shape where "the guardrail may have been silenced, its dependency may have failed, or the attacker may have learned to evade it." The chapter's deviation table names the routing: **"Rate down while overall volume steady → Guardrail failure or evasion → ML on-call + security + circuit-breaker check,"** and when the direction occurs on a control that credits residual on a register row, "Register-side flag on `control_status`; residual state recomputed" — i.e., it is a control-availability event. The engineering discipline: wire deviation in both directions, with the down direction carrying its own routing and its own register effect. A failing-open classifier that shows as a quiet metric is exactly the shape this rule is written to catch.
- D. Only cost and latency anomalies are two-sided; guardrail firing rate is one-sided by convention.

## 6. A team ships a PII-egress detector as a deviation row keyed to the rolling-baseline firing rate: alarm when the rate is > 3σ above the 24-hour baseline, sustained 15 minutes. They do not have an absolute-rule row; they argue "rate-based detection is more robust than any-fire alerts." Per chapter 03, what are the two things wrong with this shape, and what routing does the row require?

- A. Nothing — a tuned deviation threshold is more robust than an absolute rule.
- B. The team should drop the sustained-window requirement.
- C. Chapter 03 §"PII / sensitive-data leakage — the absolute-rule threshold" is explicit. First, **PII / sensitive-data egress is an absolute-rule threshold** — "any confirmed egress is S1, regardless of rate." The firing-rate deviation row is a **secondary S2 row** on the same register-row identifier, not a substitute; any confirmed positive on the sample-labelling queue trips S1 immediately and the register row's residual moves to `control-defeated` until the pathway is remediated. Second, **the routing must include the privacy owner by name** — "on-call + security + DPO (or the equivalent privacy-officer role) + governance-council digest. This is the row where the routing must include the privacy owner by name." A deviation-only shape without an absolute-rule branch allows a single confirmed leak to pass without S1 routing, which is exactly what a data-protection auditor asks about ("how did you know the leak happened; how quickly did you know; who did you tell; what did you do"); every column of the signal contract answers one part of that question, and an absolute-rule branch is the only shape that answers "how quickly did you know."
- D. Chapter 03 does not require absolute-rule thresholds for PII; the deviation shape is sufficient.

## 7. A team's near-miss counter aggregates every guardrail fire over severity S into a per-register-row weekly count and surfaces the number on the mod-112 digest. A product manager points out that false positives on a noisy classifier are inflating the count, making the trend uninformative. The team argues they should raise the severity threshold to filter noise. Per chapter 03, what discipline is missing from the counter, and which peer track owns the depth of that discipline?

- A. Nothing — raising the severity threshold is the correct response.
- B. The team should remove the near-miss counter entirely.
- C. Chapter 03 §"Near-miss data — counting the harm that did not happen" and §"The false-positive discipline" pin the discipline: **near-miss counts are only useful if false positives on the guardrail do not swamp them.** A weekly labelling review — sample the fires, label true / false positive, feed the calibration loop and the near-miss adjustment — is "not optional. Without it, the near-miss count is a guardrail-noise count, and the trend is uninformative." The near-miss counter has to *exclude* fires whose `false_positive: true` field has been set by the labelling loop; raising the severity threshold is a different lever and does not fix the FP contamination. The **depth** of the labelling review (LLM-as-judge calibration, human-in-loop protocol, inter-rater reliability) belongs to `ai-eval-engineer` at **peer L30**; the risk-side hook — that near-miss counts feeding the register are calibrated and that the record has a `false_positive` field the loop writes to — is owned at L25 here. §"The boundary to `ai-eval-engineer` on the app-eval online loop" is explicit on this seam.
- D. The calibration loop belongs entirely to this module's L25 scope; there is no peer boundary.

## 8. A provider is preparing an EU AI Act Article 72 post-market monitoring report. The draft is a 40-page narrative PDF with screenshots of dashboards, authored by the system owner over two weeks. The provider plans to compose the next report the same way, next quarter. A reviewer blocks the submission. Per chapter 04, what is the specific shape failure, and what is the chapter's rule for how the report must be generated?

- A. Nothing — a hand-composed narrative is the standard shape for a regulatory report.
- B. The team should condense the narrative to 10 pages and resubmit.
- C. Chapter 04 §"Motivation" names the shape failure directly: **"The report is composed once, at submission time. Somebody sits down and writes a narrative document referencing screenshots of dashboards; there is no live data model behind the report; the report cannot be regenerated on the next quarter without repeating the composition. The auditor asks 'where does the datum in this paragraph come from' and the trail dies."** The chapter's rule is the inverse: **"build the plan and the monitors as one artifact, and produce the report as a reduction over the register and the monitor pipeline. The report is a *view* over live data, not a bespoke composition."** §"Generating the report from the register + monitors" pins the shape — for each register row in scope, materialise residual-state history and signal-tripped log; for each signal aggregate tripped-count and sustained-trip stats; for each incident materialise its record and Article 73 notification; for each Chapter III obligation list the register rows that cite it. The materialisation is a script; running it at any point in time produces the same document from the same underlying data. §"Anti-patterns" also names "the narrative report" explicitly — "a quarterly PDF composed by hand, with screenshots of dashboards; no traceable data model behind it. Fix: generate the report as a reduction over the register + monitor pipeline."
- D. Narrative reports are fine as long as the plan is formal.

## 9. A quarter passes with no S1 signal trips. The team's Article 73 wire has not fired once during the period. A junior risk engineer argues that the Article 73 log entry for the period is "empty" and the wire does not need to be exercised until a real serious incident occurs. Per chapter 04, what is the chapter's framing of this position, and what does the Article 72 report's section 9 require?

- A. Nothing — if no serious incident fired, there is nothing to log.
- B. The team should defer the wire until an incident forces it.
- C. Chapter 04 §"Anti-patterns" names this position as the **"Article 73 wire that only runs in fire drills"** anti-pattern — "the provider has drafted an Article 73 notification once, as a rehearsal, and never revisits the wire. Fix: rehearse the wire on every S1 trip, whether or not the incident is serious." §"Dovetail with Article 73" is explicit that the wire has five steps (monitor trips → incident candidate opens → triage decides serious/not-serious → notification drafted if serious → Article 72 report captures the notification), and the engineering discipline is that every S1 trip walks the wire regardless of the triage outcome. Section 9 of the report captures "every serious incident reported under Article 73 during the period, keyed to the outbound notification and the authority notified"; for a clean period with no real S1, a **rehearsal entry** against a synthetic payload is the correct shape (exercise 04 pins the rehearsal explicitly). A clean period with an un-rehearsed wire is not the same as a tested wire — the wire has to be exercised, logged, and timed against the Article 73 deadline on a cadence, not only when it has to fire for real.
- D. Article 73 wire rehearsals are optional once the plan is signed off.

## 10. An alert fires for `SC-CS-INJ-01` (prompt-injection firing rate). The on-call acknowledges it in `#ml-oncall`, performs a rollback, and resolves the thread. The register row is not updated; the security team learns about it the next day via a separate SIEM correlation; the governance-council digest the following week shows no trip. Per chapter 05, which three specific anti-patterns from the chapter are present, and what is the "single source of truth" property the correct shape must satisfy?

- A. Nothing — if the on-call resolved the incident, the register and digest do not need to learn.
- B. The team should post a second notification to `#governance` manually.
- C. Chapter 05 §"Anti-patterns" names all three. (i) **"The Slack-only alert"** — "the alert reaches `#ml-oncall`; nothing else knows it happened. Fix: fan-out from the tripped-signal record; the channel notification is one derivative view, not the source of truth." (ii) **"Parallel dashboards"** — the on-call, security, and council each read a different query of a different data source; in this case the security team's SIEM correlation is a divergent-truth lookup rather than a derivation from the tripped-signal record. "Fix: all views query the single tripped-signal record and the register." (iii) **"The re-typed digest / un-propagated event"** — a tripped signal that does not propagate to the governance-council digest is the same failure as the re-typed digest (both leave the council view divergent from the record). The **single source of truth property** (§"What 'same source of truth' means, precisely") is: **"for any signal-tripped event, there is *one* record — the tripped-signal record filed against the signal contract row identifier — and every downstream artifact (on-call page, security ticket, governance-council digest line, Article 72 report entry, register-row update, evidence-manifest pointer) is derived from that one record."** Operating rules: no re-typing, no silent muting, no parallel dashboards, corrections propagate. The dispatcher does not embed metric values in the notification body — it embeds the record identifier and lets each audience view fetch from the record; that is what makes corrections propagate when a trip is later re-labelled false positive.
- D. The three audiences are expected to maintain independent views; convergence is not required.

## 11. A team ships the routing dispatcher with per-row conditional logic hard-coded in the dispatcher: an `if contract_id == "SC-CS-INJ-01": route_to_security()` branch for each row needing special handling. When the signal contract adds a row, the dispatcher is patched. Per chapter 05, what is the correct separation between the signal contract and the dispatcher, and why does the hard-coded shape fail the audit?

- A. Nothing — hard-coding per-row routing in the dispatcher is cleaner than config-driven dispatch.
- B. The team should merge the signal contract into the dispatcher's source file.
- C. Chapter 05 §"The routing table — the concrete contract" pins the separation: **"The severity is set on the signal contract; the routing table is applied uniformly; per-row exceptions are recorded in the contract row itself."** The per-severity routing table (S1 / S2 / S3) is applied by the dispatcher *uniformly*; per-row exceptions are data on the row (chapter 01's `alert_routing`, `owner_on_call`, `escalation_path` fields), not branches in the dispatcher's code. The exercise-05 rule restates this: "severity is set on the contract, applied uniformly by the dispatcher. Per-row exceptions are on the row. Do not let the dispatcher's code encode per-row exceptions — the code is generic, the exceptions are data." The hard-coded shape fails because (a) a threshold or routing change becomes a code diff instead of a contract-row diff, breaking the chapter-01 versioning discipline where "every threshold change is a diff and a reviewer" on the contract; (b) the single-source-of-truth property requires that corrections and policy changes propagate from the record / contract; code-level branches hide the policy from the row; (c) the chapter-05 anti-pattern probe explicitly checks for this separation.
- D. The dispatcher's code is the right place for all policy decisions.

## 12. A team adopts Fiddler AI and, over six months, begins treating Fiddler's defaults as the organisation's policy on drift, bias, and explainability. When the team evaluates replacing Fiddler with Arthur AI, they realise they cannot articulate which thresholds, slice definitions, and residual-effect mappings are *theirs* versus *Fiddler's* out-of-the-box defaults. Per chapter 06, which anti-pattern does this match, where does `ai-risk-engineer` (L25) ownership sit regardless of stack, and where does the peer boundary to `ai-infra-mlops` (L25) draw the line?

- A. Nothing — if a platform ships sensible defaults, adopting them *is* the policy.
- B. The team should switch to Holistic AI and adopt its defaults instead.
- C. Chapter 06 §"Motivation" names the anti-pattern directly: **"Vendor-shaped scope creep. A team adopts Fiddler or Arthur or Holistic AI and, because the platform *can* do drift, quality, fairness, explainability, and reporting, gradually stops distinguishing what the platform provides from what the team owns. When the platform is later swapped or its output is contested, the team cannot separate its own opinion from the vendor's default."** The engineering fix is to write the boundaries down. **`ai-risk-engineer` (L25) owns** the signal semantics (what the number means), the register-field mapping (what the number updates), the cadence choice defended per row, the threshold semantics defended against the harm, the signal contract sign-off and register effect, the routing decisions per severity, and the evidence-pointer taxonomy — "the signal contract and the register are yours; the pipeline underneath is bought or built by the MLOps track." **The peer boundary to `ai-infra-mlops` (L25)** covers the pipeline *as an engineering system*: metric emitters, metric-store integration, batch schedulers / streaming windows / backfill orchestration, detector configuration and tuning for pipeline noise, pipeline SLOs (detector uptime, FP-rate targets), integration with observability platforms, and data retention / storage / access at the pipeline level — "neither can change the identifier without notifying the other," and pipeline SLO breaches are consumed back as a control-availability signal on the register. Writing those columns down on every contract row is the discipline that "stops the ownership fog described at the start of the chapter."
- D. The commercial platform's defaults are the correct level-25 policy by construction.

## Answer key

1. **C.** Chapter 01 §"Why an ad-hoc dashboard does not count as a monitor" — a monitor must satisfy all five conditions (defined signal, threshold, cadence, routing with deterministic register-side effect, evidence trail); a dashboard fails at threshold / routing / evidence and is not credited on register rows. §"Motivation" names the "ad-hoc dashboard" failure mode in the same frame.
2. **C.** Chapter 01 §"What a signal contract is" — `deferral_boundary` is one of fifteen required columns; a row missing any column is a *draft* and not shippable. §"What the contract is *not*" and chapter 06 §"Motivation" and §"Deferral contract" pin that the peer boundaries to `ai-infra-mlops` (L25) and `ai-eval-engineer` (L30) are the two most commonly muddled; writing them down per row is the discipline that forecloses ownership fog.
3. **C.** Chapter 02 §"Types of drift, and which metric fits which" (per-feature KS misses joint shifts; aggregate metrics smooth per-slice drift) and §"Per-slice monitoring — the discipline that is easy to skip" (per-slice `signal_semantics`, per-slice threshold, register effect on the fairness-tagged mod-106 row, fairness-liaison routing). Skipping per-slice monitoring is a named fairness-audit finding.
4. **C.** Chapter 02 §"The working toolset" and its rule-of-thumb table — whylogs for tabular / per-feature drift, Evidently for batch reports with pass/fail test suites, Alibi Detect for multivariate / embedding / online / change-point / outlier detectors. "You will likely run all three; each closes a different set of rows in the contract."
5. **C.** Chapter 03 §"Deviation is a two-sided signal" — rate up *and* rate down are both signals; rate-down-while-volume-steady is the shape that catches silenced guardrails and evasion. When the direction hits a control that credits residual on a register row, it is a control-availability event (register-side flag on `control_status`, residual recomputed), with ML on-call + security + circuit-breaker-check routing.
6. **C.** Chapter 03 §"PII / sensitive-data leakage — the absolute-rule threshold" — any confirmed positive is S1 regardless of rate; the rate-deviation row is a secondary S2 row on the same register-row identifier; the routing must include the DPO by name, with security and governance-council digest on the fan-out. The absolute-rule branch is what answers "how quickly did you know" in a data-protection audit.
7. **C.** Chapter 03 §"Near-miss data" and §"The false-positive discipline" — the near-miss counter must exclude labelled false positives; without the labelling loop, the near-miss trend is a guardrail-noise trend. The **depth** of the labelling review (LLM-as-judge calibration, human-in-loop protocol, inter-rater reliability) belongs to `ai-eval-engineer` at peer L30 per §"The boundary to `ai-eval-engineer`"; the risk-side hook (the `false_positive` field on the record and the propagation into the counter's read path) is owned at L25 here.
8. **C.** Chapter 04 §"Motivation" ("the report is composed once, at submission time"), §"Generating the report from the register + monitors" (the report is a deterministic reduction), and §"Anti-patterns" (the "narrative report" entry). Running the reducer at any point in time produces the same document from the same underlying data; manual composition is a red flag.
9. **C.** Chapter 04 §"Anti-patterns" ("the Article 73 wire that only runs in fire drills"), §"Dovetail with Article 73" (the five-step wire monitor → candidate → triage → notification → report), and §"The report shape" section 9. Rehearsal entries against synthetic payloads are first-class entries in the Article 73 log; the wire must be exercised on cadence, not only on real serious incidents.
10. **C.** Chapter 05 §"Anti-patterns" (Slack-only alert; parallel dashboards; re-typed / un-propagated event) and §"What 'same source of truth' means, precisely" (one tripped-signal record per event; every downstream artifact derived from that one record; no re-typing, no silent muting, no parallel dashboards, corrections propagate). The dispatcher embeds the record identifier, not metric values, in the notification body.
11. **C.** Chapter 05 §"The routing table — the concrete contract" ("severity is set on the signal contract; the routing table is applied uniformly; per-row exceptions are recorded in the contract row itself") plus the chapter-01 versioning discipline ("every threshold change is a diff and a reviewer" on the contract, not a code diff). Hard-coding per-row branches in the dispatcher moves policy out of the row and breaks the versioning / single-source-of-truth properties.
12. **C.** Chapter 06 §"Motivation" ("vendor-shaped scope creep"), §"The peer boundary to `ai-infra-mlops` at L25" (the ownership split table — signal semantics / register mapping / cadence / threshold / sign-off / routing / evidence-pointer taxonomy on the risk-engineer side; metric emitters / metric-store integration / schedulers / detector plumbing / pipeline SLOs / observability integration / pipeline-level retention on the MLOps side; two shared surfaces — the monitor identifier and the pipeline SLO), and §"Deferral contract — what this module does *not* own." Writing the boundary on every contract row is the chapter's prescribed fix.
