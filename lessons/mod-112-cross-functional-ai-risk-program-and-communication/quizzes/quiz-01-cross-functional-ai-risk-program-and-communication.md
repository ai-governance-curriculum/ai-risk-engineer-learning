# Quiz 01 — Cross-Functional AI Risk Programme and Communication

**Format:** 12 multiple-choice questions · **Open-book.** Use the chapters as reference.

Each question tests a *concept* from the module, not phrasing. The answer key at the bottom cites the chapter section the answer comes from.

---

## 1. A team at level 25 has a complete harm model, a scored register, guardrail-effectiveness numbers, monitor dashboards, and incident-response playbooks — every artefact mod-102 through mod-111 names. An ISO/IEC 42001 lead auditor arrives on-site, reviews the artefacts, and files the finding "the risk-management process is documented but not operating." What specific failure mode does this match per chapter 01, and what is the discipline that closes it?

- A. Nothing — if every artefact exists, the programme is operating by definition; the audit finding is wrong.
- B. The team should have hired an external consultant to walk the auditor through the artefacts; the finding is a communication issue, not a programme issue.
- C. Chapter 01 §"Motivation" names this verbatim as the **"all-artefact, no-cadence"** failure mode — every required artefact exists but nothing is reviewed on a schedule, nothing is signed off, and the artefacts drift out of alignment with the running system. §"The four-tier cadence" is the discipline that closes it: the programme runs a weekly 30-minute operational triage (register updates, monitor alerts, incidents, red-team hits), a bi-weekly 60-minute working session (treatment work and guardrail-effectiveness deltas), a monthly 60-90-minute programme review (register aggregation, monitoring digest, incident summary), and a quarterly 2-3-hour council-facing review. Each cadence tier has a defined attendee list and a defined set of artefacts refreshed on that cadence. **Cadence drives artefact refresh; artefacts anchor decisions; decisions route through a named sign-off graph.** Skipping any one tier collapses work into the next tier down — the weekly becomes triage-plus-strategy, the monthly becomes triage-plus-council-prep — and something falls off. The sibling failure §"Motivation" names is **"all-meeting, no-artefact"** — meetings produce minutes that produce action items that produce more meetings, but no versioned artefact carries the current position on the risk and a new joiner cannot answer "what does this programme own right now." The four-tier cadence is the specific fix for both failures: cadence without artefacts is theatre, and artefacts without cadence are shelfware.
- D. The team should merge the four cadence tiers into a single monthly meeting to reduce overhead; the auditor's finding is a scheduling preference, not a substantive issue.

## 2. A council pre-read arrives with 40 slides. The council walks through them in order, nods at each, and the meeting ends. Three "decisions" the risk engineer wanted taken are deferred to the next quarter because "we did not have time to discuss them." Per chapter 02, what failure mode is this, and what is the load-bearing discipline that converts a pre-read from a report into a forcing function?

- A. Nothing — a 40-slide deep-read is the appropriate shape for a quarterly council; the deferrals are a normal council workflow.
- B. The team should have moved the three decisions to a separate ad-hoc meeting; the quarterly is for information, not decisions.
- C. Chapter 02 §"Motivation" names this verbatim as the **"read-out session"** failure — the risk engineer walks the council through 40 slides of what happened last quarter, and no decision is taken. §"The pre-read deck — 'one decision per slide'" pins the discipline: the pre-read carries three kinds of slide in this order — 2-4 **context slides** (programme scope, systems in scope, register-health heat map, incident summary); **decision slides**, typically 3-6, each asking the council **a single yes/no or A/B/C question** with the evidence and the recommendation; and 2-4 **signal slides** (monitoring digest, obligations landing next quarter, staffing posture — no decision asked). The load-bearing rule is **one decision per slide**; a slide that says "and there is also this other thing the council should know" is a signal slide, not a decision slide, and mixing them produces decisions that were not really made — the meeting nods and moves on. §"Common failure modes and their fixes" pins the fix directly: cap decision slides at 6; move context into pre-read only; use meeting time for decisions. The sibling failure §"Motivation" names is **"ambush decision"** — a decision put in front of the council with no evidence pack, so the decision is deferred and the programme stalls — and §"Common failure modes" fixes it by requiring a 5-7-day pre-read distribution deadline with no decisions added the day of. Both failures are **pre-read failures**: when the pre-read is right, the meeting decides. The pre-read is not a report; it is a *forcing function*.
- D. The team should circulate the 40-slide deck the day of the meeting so the council reads it live; this focuses attention and prevents deferrals.

## 3. A risk engineer's council decision slide reads "Approve promotion of the agentic ticket-triage variant from T1 to T2," cites the register rows, names the recommendation ("approve"), and nothing else. The council approves. Six months later a monitor fires on the system and the team argues for a week about whether to revert, because no reversibility criterion was ever pre-registered. Per chapter 02, what seven-field template did the slide fail, and which specific field would have prevented the week-long argument?

- A. Nothing — reversibility is a release-engineering concern, not a council-decision-slide concern.
- B. The team should have added the reversibility to the minutes rather than the slide; the slide shape was adequate.
- C. Chapter 02 §"The decision-slide template" pins **seven fields, in the same order, on every decision slide**: (i) **Decision requested** (one imperative sentence); (ii) **Scope** (which system, which population, which time window); (iii) **Evidence pointers** (direct links to register rows, evaluation reports, monitor extracts, red-team findings); (iv) **Options and recommendation** (A/B/C or approve/defer/reject; the recommendation in ≤ 3 lines of rationale); (v) **Residual-risk claim** (the one-line residual claim with the level after the decision if approved); (vi) **Escalation dependency** (any decision this depends on); (vii) **Rollback / reversibility** (if approved, how the decision is reversed and under what condition). "**If any of the seven fields is empty, the slide is not ready for the council. Pull it, close the gap, and hold it for the next cycle.**" The specific field that would have prevented the week-long argument is **Rollback / reversibility** — chapter 02 §"Common failure modes and their fixes" names **"missing rollback"** directly: approved decisions with no reversibility clause; the fix is that rollback/reversibility is a required decision-slide field and a decision without one is not ready. Chapter 04 §"Rollback criteria" pins the same shape from the tiering side: rollback is *mechanical* (a config flip), not a human decision at incident time; if revert requires a human decision, the criterion has not been pre-registered properly. The seven fields together are what turn a council decision into a durable, testable, reversible commitment; losing any one field is how programmes acquire decisions they cannot unwind.
- D. The team should have skipped the council-decision route entirely and let the on-call revert without pre-registration; speed beats formality.

## 4. A newly authored model card lists the model's intended use, cites evaluation numbers from mod-104 and mod-108, lists known fairness-slice deltas from mod-106, and names the DPIA from mod-107. The "Bias, Risks and Limitations" section reads: "The model has residual limitations; we continue to monitor." An EU AI Act Article 13 transparency review returns the card non-conforming. Per chapter 03, which card component is load-bearing and what are its three required parts?

- A. Nothing — if the card lists evaluations and limitations, Article 13 transparency is satisfied; the review is a procedural technicality.
- B. The team should have moved the residual language to the top of the card; the problem is slide order, not content.
- C. Chapter 03 §"6. Residual-risk claim" pins the residual claim as the **load-bearing** card component — "**a first-class section** — either 'Bias, Risks and Limitations' or a dedicated 'Residual Risk' subsection." The working shape has **three required parts**: (i) **the residual level** — on the specific population, cohort, and use case evaluated, per the programme's rubric; (ii) **the monitoring conditions under which the claim holds** — named monitor IDs from mod-109 remaining within named thresholds; (iii) **the pre-registered invalidation triggers** — specific conditions that revoke the claim and the tier/mitigated state the system reverts to (per this module ch 04). The card's wording — "residual limitations; we continue to monitor" — is exactly the failure §"Motivation" names as the **"card without residual claim"**: describes evaluations and limitations but never asserts the residual position — what risk remains after mitigations, on which population, in which context, and how monitoring is meant to catch violation of that claim. "**The claim is testable, tied to monitoring, and reversible. A residual-risk claim without an invalidation condition is not defensible.**" The chapter's sibling failure is the **"marketing card"** (sections carry adjectives instead of numbers — "guardrails performed well" instead of "G-INJ-01 attained 0.93 F1"); the fix in both cases is that the card is a **projection of the register**, not a fresh authoring pass — every risk-slice section is a view over evidence authored upstream, with numbers, slice definitions, caveats, and the three-part residual claim carried through.
- D. The team should have omitted the "Bias, Risks and Limitations" section entirely; a card without that section cannot be found non-conforming on it.

## 5. A team writes a deployment-tiering document for a customer-support RAG assistant that lifts CBRN-uplift language, autonomous-replication triggers, and self-exfiltration categories directly from the Anthropic RSP text into the document. The document is reviewed and returned as "not defensible at team scope." Per chapter 04, which specific failure mode is this, and where does the level-25 / level-40 boundary actually sit on dangerous-capability categories?

- A. Nothing — copying from the RSP is the appropriate way to align team-scope tiering with frontier practice; the reviewer is being pedantic.
- B. The team should expand the CBRN-uplift language into greater detail; the problem is depth, not scope.
- C. Chapter 04 §"Motivation" names this verbatim as **"cargo-culted frontier thresholds"** — a team lifts CBRN-uplift language or autonomous-replication triggers directly from the RSP or Preparedness text into a document for a customer-support assistant; the thresholds are irrelevant and the tiering is not defensible. §"The level-40 boundary — what escalates to `agentic-safety-engineer`" pins what belongs above level 25: (i) **frontier capability categories** — CBRN uplift, autonomous replication, R&D acceleration, self-exfiltration, deceptive alignment — "**these are not team-scope enterprise thresholds**. If a deployment enters territory where any of these categories are plausibly in scope, the tiering escalates"; (ii) **frontier evaluation harnesses** (RSP / Preparedness / FSF-associated); (iii) **multi-model / cross-agent dangerous capability evaluation**; (iv) **ruling on frontier-tier deployment consequences**. The team-scope tiering document should **name** these escalations explicitly — a section titled "Escalation to `agentic-safety-engineer` (L40)" lists the conditions and the evidence packaged for the hand-off. The productive L25 contribution is a **pre-registered, testable, reversible** tiering document **sized to your actual system**: team-scope tripwires are injection-defeat spikes, novel red-team hits, fairness-slice divergences, canary leaks, autonomy escalation beyond authorised tool calls, supply-chain changes — not frontier capability categories. The chapter's sibling failures are **"post-hoc tiering"** (the demo goes well, someone asks "what tier is this?" and the team writes the tier to fit what shipped — no forcing function) and **"undocumented rollback"** (promotion criterion named but no rollback criterion, so the team argues in real time when a monitor fires). The deferral contract from mod-101 ch 06 is explicit here: contribute at team scope; do not own frontier-scale thresholds (L40) and do not own the enterprise tiering policy (L60).
- D. The team should keep the frontier-lab language but mark it "aspirational"; aspirational thresholds are defensible.

## 6. A tier-promotion block for a T2 → T3 move names the thresholds ("register clean; guardrails above F1 0.92; red-team suite green"), the rollback criteria, and the tripwires, but every threshold is a *fresh number* chosen in a half-day working session and not tied back to any underlying artefact. A release engineer tries to wire the rollback into the release pipeline and cannot — the F1 threshold "0.92" has no referent in the evidence pack. Per chapter 04, what is the discipline that the block violated, and what makes rollback criteria mechanically enforceable?

- A. Nothing — fresh numbers chosen by a working session are the correct way to set tier thresholds; the release engineer should have asked for clarification.
- B. The team should have added more decimal places to the F1 threshold; precision is the issue, not provenance.
- C. Chapter 04 §"1. Pre-registered thresholds" pins the discipline: the risk engineer contributes threshold values by **reading them off existing artefacts**, not by authoring fresh numbers. Every threshold **cites its source artefact by reference**: register aggregation report for "all rows for this system at or below appetite"; guardrail effectiveness report for "G-01 through G-N attain ≥ [F1 threshold] on the current retention corpus"; adversarial-ML delta for perturbation-tolerance; fairness-slice report for per-slice delta; privacy report for canary-extraction rate; red-team programme for suite-closure state; monitor spec for monitor-health; incident log for open-severity state. "**The tiering document does not restate the numbers, it pins them by reference. When the source updates, the threshold updates.**" On rollback, §"2. Rollback criteria" pins that every tier promotion carries a **pre-registered rollback criterion** authored as the **inverse of the promotion threshold with tolerance**: if the promotion threshold is "monitor rate < 0.3 percent," the rollback is "monitor rate > 0.5 percent for 3 consecutive days" — the 3-day guard prevents single-day noise from flipping the tier. "**Rollback is *mechanical* — a config flip in the release pipeline. If revert requires a human decision, the criterion has not been pre-registered properly.**" The worked T2 → T3 example in §"A worked short-form tier promotion" enumerates nine threshold categories (register, guardrails, adversarial-ML, fairness, privacy, red-team, monitoring, incidents, cards) each cited to a source artefact and five rollback triggers each specifying the exact metric, the exact threshold, and the pre-registered duration. The release engineer can wire the rollback into the release pipeline because every criterion is already a monitor-ID-plus-threshold with a documented duration guard.
- D. The team should have deferred the rollback criteria to be authored after the first real incident; pre-registration is premature optimisation.

## 7. A framework crosswalk is a document of paragraphs — "we address NIST AI RMF MEASURE 2.5 through our red-team programme" — with no direct pointer to a specific evidence artefact and no version. An ISO/IEC 42001 lead auditor asks which specific artefact satisfies MEASURE 2.5 and the programme has to search for 20 minutes. Per chapter 05, what failure mode is this, what is the "one artefact, many standards" pattern that resolves it, and what are the two load-bearing properties of a proper crosswalk row?

- A. Nothing — prose crosswalks are the standard enterprise shape; a 20-minute search is within normal audit response time.
- B. The team should have added a footnote citing the register; the paragraph shape is otherwise correct.
- C. Chapter 05 §"Motivation" names three failure modes that compose here: (i) the **"prose crosswalk"** — a document of paragraphs with no direct artefact pointer and no version; an auditor cannot verify from the document alone that the claim holds today; (ii) the **"N-to-1 collapse"** — the same artefact (typically "the register") is pointed at from every sub-category with no per-sub-category granularity; the auditor asks which row satisfies MEASURE 2.5 and the programme has to search; (iii) the **"1-to-1 duplication"** — a separate artefact for every sub-category producing redundant evidence stores that drift and disagree. The productive shape §"Crosswalk shape" pins is **one row per evidence artefact, columns for framework citations** (RMF MEASURE / MANAGE sub-cats, ISO/IEC 42001 Annex A controls, EU AI Act articles, sector overlays, owner, last refresh). The **"one artefact, many standards" pattern** (introduced in mod-101 ch 03 and worked here) means a single artefact — a red-team run report, a guardrail effectiveness report, a monitor spec — is pointed at by *multiple* framework rows, and each pointer is **granular (row-level, section-level, version-hash pinned)**. The two load-bearing properties are: (i) **many-to-many** — one artefact points at multiple framework citations and one framework citation may be pointed at by multiple artefacts, by design; (ii) **version-hash pinned** — every artefact pointer is a hash so when the artefact refreshes the crosswalk row updates and when the framework is revised the citation column updates and any newly uncovered sub-category becomes a visible gap. The economic consequence: **authoring effort scales with the number of evidence artefacts, not with the product of artefacts and framework citations**. §"The 'one artefact, many standards' pattern — worked" cites a single guardrail-effectiveness report filing against seven distinct downstream uses (MEASURE-2.5, MEASURE-2.7, MANAGE-2.2, ISO 42001 V&V, EU AI Act Article 15, deployment-tier contract threshold, model-card "Evaluation" section).
- D. The team should have hired more analysts to maintain the prose crosswalk; the problem is staffing, not shape.

## 8. A crosswalk is populated for every artefact the programme has. An auditor compares the crosswalk to the in-scope list of RMF MEASURE sub-categories, ISO/IEC 42001 Annex A controls, and EU AI Act articles; several citations have no filled artefact pointer. The team marks them "TBD" and files the crosswalk. The audit finding: the AIMS is not operating. Per chapter 05, what is the discipline that forbids unmarked gaps, and what are the three dispositions every gap must take?

- A. Nothing — TBD is an acceptable transitional state for gaps; the auditor is applying an overly strict reading.
- B. The team should have deleted the uncovered citations from the crosswalk; a shorter crosswalk has fewer gaps.
- C. Chapter 05 §"Coverage gap detection" pins the gap-detection discipline: enumerate every in-scope MEASURE / MANAGE sub-category and every in-scope ISO/IEC 42001 Annex A control; subtract the set with at least one crosswalk row; **report the remainder as gaps**. "**A crosswalk with unmarked gaps is not shippable to audit. Every framework cell must be one of: filled, waived, or escalated.**" The **three dispositions** every gap must take: (i) **Fill** — the gap corresponds to work the programme *should* be doing; the response is to schedule the missing artefact (owner, target date, back-pointer to the crosswalk row); (ii) **Waive with rationale** — the gap corresponds to a sub-category or control that is out-of-scope for this system (e.g., an autonomous-agent-oversight control for a system with no autonomous capabilities); the waiver is **written up with rationale, dated, signed off, and stored in the crosswalk**; (iii) **Escalate** — the gap corresponds to work above level 25 (release-assurance methodology at L35; control-library architecture at L50; programme-scale policy at L60); the gap is marked as escalated **with the mod-101 ch 06 escalation contract cited**, and the L25 role continues to maintain the citation while the higher-level work happens. "TBD" is not one of the three — it is the exact shape §"Motivation" names as non-defensible. §"Maintenance discipline" wires the gap register into cadence: monthly hash check on every artefact pointer; per-framework-revision citation refresh; per-system-change re-check; per-audit-cycle regeneration; CI check on the crosswalk file ensures every citation cell resolves, every referenced artefact exists at the pinned hash, and no citation is left blank without a waiver entry.
- D. The team should have marked every gap "waived" without a rationale; waivers outnumbering fills is a normal state.

## 9. An engineer holds five credentials: two AI-governance certificates from vendor-run training courses, one industry-conference completion badge, one AI-ethics-overview certificate, and one AI-trust certificate. At enterprise interviews the portfolio is read as "breadth signalling with no evidence of applied depth." Per chapter 06, which failure mode is this, which five credential families are load-bearing in enterprise hiring, and what is the productive posture on certification choice?

- A. Nothing — five credentials signal more commitment than two; the hiring managers are undervaluing breadth.
- B. The team should add a sixth credential to further signal breadth; the issue is portfolio volume, not shape.
- C. Chapter 06 §"Motivation" names this as the **"badge collection"** failure — the engineer holds five credentials that overlap in coverage and none in depth; on a hiring page the portfolio reads as breadth signalling with no evidence of applied depth. The sibling failure §"Motivation" names is **"wrong-programme investment"** — six months and several thousand pounds / dollars in a credential marginal to the target employer's hiring signal, often because the engineer picked the credential most publicised that year rather than the one enterprise hiring actually filters on. §"The five credential families relevant to level 25" names the load-bearing families: (i) **IAPP AI Governance Professional (AIGP)** — the breadth credential for AI governance; often listed as "required" or "strongly preferred" in enterprise AI-governance postings; (ii) **ForHumanity Independent AI Auditor** — audit-side depth; particularly meaningful for engineers moving into third-party audit / assurance work; (iii) **BABL AI Algorithm Bias Auditor** — depth specifically in fairness/bias auditing; especially meaningful if the target employer is subject to a bias-audit regulation (NYC LL 144 is the prominent example); (iv) **ISO/IEC 42001 Lead Implementer (LI) and Lead Auditor (LA)** — standard depth for ISO-mature organisations or organisations targeting 42001 certification; (v) **IAPP CIPP/US or CIPP/E** — the privacy adjacency that many AI-risk-engineering roles require because privacy risk sits inside AI risk. The productive posture: **certifications are hiring signals to specific employers**; pick the **two-to-four that are load-bearing for the target employer type** rather than collecting badges. §"Which one when — a decision table" pairs employer / role profile with portfolio shape (enterprise ISO-mature → AIGP + 42001 LI + CIPP; audit firm → ForHumanity + 42001 LA + AIGP; NYC LL 144-covered → BABL AI + AIGP + ForHumanity; L50 target → AIGP + 42001 LI and LA + CIPP). §"What is *not* on the list" is also load-bearing: **named engagement with a public evaluation programme** (MLCommons, UK/US AISI, open-source red-team) and **publications/speaking at recognised venues** (NeurIPS, ICML, FAccT, RSA, DEF CON) are hiring signals **certifications complement rather than replace**.
- D. The team should pick the most expensive certification and drop the other four; cost signals rigour.

## 10. An on-call engineer declares an incident; the incident is classified by severity; routing is set; a post-mortem is filed. The engineer does not open the decision log or author a decision-log entry. Six months later an auditor asks "who authorised the above-appetite acceptance that was referenced in the incident post-mortem" and the team searches minutes and finds the answer is in a meeting record but not in any queryable ledger. Per chapter 02, what is the specific failure mode, where does the decision log actually live, and what is the relationship between the log and the meeting minutes?

- A. Nothing — the minutes are the authoritative record; the decision log is an optional convenience.
- B. The team should have added a PDF of the minutes to the register; the minutes are the ledger.
- C. Chapter 02 §"Common failure modes and their fixes" names this verbatim as **"log-in-minutes"** — decisions recorded only in minutes; no queryable log; the fix is that **the decision log is a separate artefact in the register repo; every council-approved decision is an entry**. §"The decision log" pins what the log is: **the single ledger of every decision the council has taken** — **not the meeting minutes and not the pre-read**; it is the **durable, queryable list**. One entry per council decision, in a defined YAML schema with these fields: `decision_id`, `title`, `requested_by`, `scope`, `decision` (approve / approve-with-conditions / defer / reject), `conditions`, `decided_at`, `council_members_present`, `dissents`, `evidence_pack_ref`, `evidence_pack_hash`, `minutes_ref`, `linked_register_rows`, `linked_obligations`, `followups`, `supersedes` (if any), `next_review`. The log **lives in the register repo (or the GRC platform), not in a slide deck**. Its purpose is to be **queryable**: "give me every above-appetite acceptance in the last four quarters"; "give me every decision tied to a specific system"; "give me every decision linked to NIST AI RMF MEASURE 2.5." §"Minutes that survive audit" draws the complementary role — the minutes are **the record of the meeting** and audit reads them to confirm the council met, had the required attendees, considered the pre-read, and reached the decisions the log records. The three properties that make minutes audit-durable: (i) they **reference** the pre-read and one-pager by hash; (ii) they **distinguish** decisions from discussion; (iii) they carry **sign-off from more than one attendee**. The log is where the decisions live as data; the minutes are where the meeting itself is attested to. Decision-log discipline is what mod-103 ch 06's sign-off routing looks like at council scope — the entries in the log are the register's council-level sign-offs made queryable.
- D. The team should have skipped the decision-log entry if the decision was obvious; brevity is the audit-trail virtue.

## 11. A programme has a monitor that fires on an above-appetite fairness delta on a protected-attribute slice. The monitor has been muted for three quarters without appearing on any council agenda. The programme's register shows the row as "controlled; no exception required." Per chapter 02 and chapter 01, which specific failure modes compose here, and what two mechanisms would have forced the mute onto the council?

- A. Nothing — mute of a monitor is a technical decision that belongs to the on-call rotation, not the council.
- B. The team should have deleted the monitor rather than muting it; deletion is more transparent than mute.
- C. Chapter 02 §"Common failure modes and their fixes" names **"silent above-appetite"** — above-appetite rows aggregated in the pre-read but no decision requested; fix: **make above-appetite acceptance a forced decision slide when appetite is breached; no drift by silence**. The sibling failure mode here is the **unreviewed mute**: chapter 01 §"The decisions the programme owns" decision matrix explicitly handles **"Mute / retire a monitor (mod-109)"** — owner is the risk engineer + monitor owner **with justification recorded** — but **mute of a control-defeated tripwire escalates to the risk council**, not the on-call rotation. Chapter 04 §"2. Rollback criteria" compounds this: **"Mute of any T3+ tripwire monitor without risk-council approval"** is itself a rollback trigger — the system automatically reverts to a lower tier. The two mechanisms that force the mute onto the council: (i) **the forced-decision-slide rule** — when appetite is breached, a decision slide is required at the next council (not optional); the one-pager's "number of rows above appetite; delta since last council" field is where the council reads the aggregate; (ii) **the sign-off routing escalation** — chapter 01 §"The sign-off routing graph" pins that system-level decisions (mute of a tripwire monitor) require three signatures (risk engineer + system owner + security partner) with **risk council countersign on top** for programme-level decisions; chapter 02's decision log carries one entry per mute with `conditions`, `decided_at`, and `next_review` — a muted monitor with no log entry is a governance gap the crosswalk gap-detection in chapter 05 would surface. The sibling failures in chapter 02 §"Common failure modes" that compose with this — "log-in-minutes" and "evidence-hash absence" — mean the mute is not only off-council but also unrecorded in any queryable form. The discipline that closes it: **muted monitors are how programmes go silent**; the mute is a council-owned decision with a reversibility clause (what un-mutes the monitor), recorded in the decision log and surfaced in the one-pager's register-health position.
- D. The team should have retired the monitor without mute; silent retirement is cleaner than mute.

## 12. A level-25 risk engineer running the full cadence is approached about an autonomous-agent incident where a planner model spawned a sub-agent outside its declared topology and used credentials outside their intended scope. The engineer runs the full RCA, authors the post-mortem, updates the register, refreshes the crosswalk, and files the council decision-log entry — all unilaterally. Per chapters 04 and 06, which escalation triggers fired, and what is the correct division of labour between level 25, level 40, and level 60?

- A. Nothing — the level-25 role owns every AI incident end-to-end regardless of the capability surface.
- B. The team should have deferred the entire loop to level 60; the risk council chairs all incidents of this shape.
- C. Chapter 04 §"The level-40 boundary — what escalates to `agentic-safety-engineer`" pins the frontier triggers: (i) the deployment tier is above the enterprise's non-frontier threshold; (ii) **autonomous action outside the authorised scope** — a plan that steps outside the declared goal, a **sub-agent spawned outside the declared topology**, **credentials used outside their intended scope on a chain of the model's own construction**; (iii) a frontier-risk category is plausibly in scope (CBRN uplift, cyber uplift with autonomous execution, autonomous replication, undermining oversight); (iv) downstream effect on model-provider-level safeguards. The scenario matches (ii) explicitly. Chapter 06 §"The career hand-off to level 35 / 40 / 50" pins the role boundaries in the same family: **level 25** owns risk-engineering across the full surface at team scope (programme cadence, charter, council packet, cards, tiering block, crosswalk); **level 35 (`ai-evaluation-engineer`)** owns release-assurance / audit-trail methodology depth — what L25 produced as artefacts, L35 packages as evidence bundles for regulator engagement and external audit; **level 40 (`agentic-safety-engineer`)** owns frontier-agent red-team methodology and dangerous-capability evaluation depth — the escalation territory mod-102 ch 04 and this module ch 04 named; **level 60 (`head-of-ai-governance`)** owns tiering policy at programme scale, cross-programme promotion authority, external commitments (regulators, customers, public), and the audit-facing statement of tiering intent. The correct division of labour: L25 continues to run the deployment-side loop (mitigation on the enterprise-deployment slice, harm-model / register update, fixture back-feed, deployment-side regulator packages) and hands off the **frontier-agent-specific RCA and the frontier-tier post-mortem section to L40**, signing onto the hand-off record; L25 ensures the L40 output is folded back into the deployment-side post-mortem (one incident with two authors on the frontier-agent section, not two incidents); any change to the enterprise tiering policy or any external commitment about deployment-tier discipline routes through L60. Chapter 04 §"What the role contributes at level 25" is explicit — L25 **contributes** to tiering (thresholds, rollback, tripwires, the tiering document at team scope); frontier-capability categories, frontier evaluation harnesses, multi-model / cross-agent dangerous-capability evaluation, and rulings on frontier-tier deployment consequences escalate to L40. The deferral contract from mod-101 ch 06 makes the boundary explicit so the L40 and L60 moves are specialisations rather than departures.
- D. The team should merge L25, L40, and L60 responsibilities into a single role; separation creates escalation overhead.
