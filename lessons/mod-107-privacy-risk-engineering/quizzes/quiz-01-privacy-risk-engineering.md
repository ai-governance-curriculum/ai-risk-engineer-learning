# Quiz 01 — Privacy-Risk Engineering for ML and LLM Systems

**Format:** 12 multiple-choice questions · **Open-book.** Use the chapters as reference.

Each question tests a *concept* from the module, not phrasing. The answer key at the bottom cites the chapter the answer comes from.

---

## 1. A team ships a classifier trained in part on an Article-9 health cohort, runs attribute-inference and model-inversion evals, and files the privacy-risk report with the single-sentence summary "the model does not leak training-set membership." A reviewer flags the report as not-shippable. Per chapter 01, what specifically did the team get wrong, and what is the shippable shape?

- A. Nothing — attribute inference and inversion together subsume the membership claim.
- B. The team should have averaged the attribute-inference advantage and the inversion quality into a single composite privacy score.
- C. The summary **collapses four empirically distinct claims into one sentence**. Chapter 01 names the four — membership inference (MIA), attribute inference, model inversion, memorisation / extraction — and is explicit that each has its own evaluation, its own metric, its own defence, and its own register row. "The model does not leak training-set membership" is specifically the MIA claim, and the team never ran a MIA eval. The shippable shape is four separate rows in the register, each with a named attack evaluation and a residual number, with the MIA row produced by Shokri-style and / or Carlini LiRA under a stated threat model.
- D. The team should run only MIA, since membership inference is the "anchor" attack.

## 2. A team's deployment is a RAG system over internal documents with no fine-tuning of the base LLM. The team argues, citing the chapter-01 applicability table, that MIA and model inversion are "n/a" on the base model but that the PII problem lives in the retrieval store. A reviewer asks the team to re-check the table. Per chapter 01, is the team's reading correct, and what does the applicability table pin for this deployment shape?

- A. The reading is wrong — MIA and inversion are always "high relevance."
- B. The team's reading is correct. The chapter-01 applicability table pins the RAG-on-internal-documents-with-no-fine-tune shape as **not-directly-on-the-base-model for MIA, attribute inference, and inversion**; the privacy risk for this shape is the *retrieval store itself*, which is the PII-pipeline problem engineered in chapter 05. The report still files rows for the attacks that are n/a — stating "n/a for this deployment shape, with the applicability-table row as the justification" — rather than silently omitting them, so the auditor sees the decision rather than guessing it.
- C. The team should still run MIA on the base model just in case.
- D. The applicability table is advisory and the team should ignore it.

## 3. A junior engineer runs a Shokri-style shadow-model MIA on the target model, reports AUC = 0.60, and claims the model has "modest membership-inference risk." Per chapter 02, what are the two specific things wrong with this reporting, and what is the shippable reporting shape?

- A. Nothing — AUC is the canonical metric for MIA.
- B. The engineer should have reported accuracy instead of AUC.
- C. Two things wrong. First, **AUC alone is misleading** — Carlini et al. (2022) showed that two attacks with identical AUC can differ by an order of magnitude on TPR at low FPR, and TPR at low FPR is the number that tracks actual disclosure risk (how many records can the attacker identify at a false-positive rate low enough to *use*). Second, the engineer ran only Shokri; the chapter pins the pair — Shokri as the baseline and **LiRA** (Carlini S&P 2022) as the attack that makes the low-FPR tail visible. The shippable shape reports AUC alongside **TPR at 1 % FPR and TPR at 0.1 % FPR** per slice (overall, small-class, outlier-decile), with both attacks on the same table so the AUC-can-lie contrast is visible.
- D. Report AUC and call it done — the chapter does not require LiRA.

## 4. A team runs LiRA and reports an overall TPR at 1 % FPR of 0.03, with a small-class slice at 0.18 and an outlier-decile slice at 0.32. They file the overall number as the residual-risk signal on the mod-103 register row and omit the slices "to keep the register clean." Per chapter 02, what is the chapter's position on slice reporting, and what does omitting the slice numbers hide?

- A. Nothing — overall is sufficient for a register row.
- B. The team should have dropped the outlier-decile slice because outliers skew the number.
- C. Chapter 02 is explicit that **MIA is not uniform across records** — training-set outliers, small classes, and rare subpopulations are the highest-vulnerability slices, and a single overall number hides population-scale disparities a governance reader must see. The reporting table *requires* slice rows alongside the overall number. Omitting the slice numbers hides a 10× higher TPR on the outlier decile (0.32 vs 0.03) behind an aggregate, which is the exact over-claim Carlini-style low-FPR reporting is designed to prevent. The shippable register row carries both the overall and the slice worst-case as two distinct residual-risk cells.
- D. Slice numbers are level-30 methodological depth and not level-25 scope.

## 5. A team wants to evaluate LLM memorisation on a model they fine-tuned on an internal corpus. They run a canary eval with 5 canaries at duplication count 10 using only the direct-completion elicitation strategy, observe zero extracted canaries, and file "extraction rate = 0." Per chapter 03, what are the two specific things wrong with this report, and what is the shippable shape?

- A. Nothing — zero elicited means zero extraction.
- B. The team should have used more canaries at higher duplication.
- C. Two things wrong. First, the prompt budget and the elicitation-strategy list are **part of the number** — chapter 03 is explicit that "extraction rate = 0" is meaningless without stating the prompt budget and the attack strategies attempted. Second, the attack list is incomplete — the shippable canary eval tries at least direct completion, **prefix-from-context** (prompting with the natural training-context prefix, which is the Carlini-2023 scaling-law lever), and **divergence prompting** (the Nasr et al. 2023 "repeat this word forever" pattern); a run that tries only direct completion has not attempted the elicitation strategies known to raise memorisation visibility. The shippable sentence is "under attack list A, prompt budget B per canary, duplication count D, extraction rate is X" — not an unqualified "0."
- D. The team should report "no memorisation possible" since the result is zero.

## 6. A team ships a per-person face-recognition classifier for a small internal use case (20 identities), returns the full probability vector from the API for "downstream flexibility," and runs an MIA baseline that shows low TPR at 1 % FPR. They file the privacy report and claim that privacy risk is adequately characterised by MIA. Per chapter 03, which attack is the chapter's "danger shape" for this deployment, and what does the chapter prescribe?

- A. MIA fully characterises the risk; the team is correct.
- B. The team should have run attribute inference on the 20 identities.
- C. Small-population classifiers where each class is a person are the **chapter-03 danger shape for model inversion**. The chapter is explicit: "the class *is* the person; a plausible reconstruction of the class is a plausible reconstruction of the person." MIA does not characterise this risk — the attacker here does not need to decide whether a record was in training; they need to *reconstruct the labelled identity* (Fredrikson CCS 2015). The report must include an inversion eval reporting both **reconstruction quality** (SSIM / LPIPS / task-specific metric) *and* **identifiability** (whether a human labeller can name the reconstruction); and the chapter names "don't expose confidence vectors on production APIs — return top-1 label only" as a primary defence directly relevant to this deployment.
- D. Model inversion is out of scope for this module.

## 7. A team trains a model with Opacus, reports ε = 100 at δ = 10⁻⁵ with the note "DP-SGD was used, so MIA cannot happen," and asks the review to credit DP-SGD as the mitigating control. Per chapter 04, what are the two specific things wrong with this parameter choice and claim, and what is the chapter's framing of (ε, δ) interpretation?

- A. Nothing — any ε is DP and that is sufficient.
- B. The team should have used ε = 0.
- C. Two things wrong. First, **ε = 100 is formally DP but practically meaningless** — chapter 04 names this as an anti-pattern ("ε = 100 is still DP. Formally true; practically useless. Very large ε provides essentially no meaningful privacy bound."). The honest shape when utility cannot be borne at single-digit ε is "DP-SGD does not meaningfully bound this scenario at achievable utility," not an inflated ε kept for utility. Second, **"DP means MIA cannot happen" is wrong** — DP-SGD is a formal property of the mechanism (the multiplicative bound in the definition), not a guarantee about outcomes; empirical MIA is still *measured* on the DP-trained model and reported as the residual-risk signal. The report defends (ε, δ) on three grounds — threat-model calibration, utility calibration, and comparability to published references — not by asserting that the (ε, δ) bound dispenses with the empirical eval.
- D. The team should keep ε = 100 since the accountant allowed it.

## 8. A team trains a DP-SGD model with Opacus using a standard PyTorch `DataLoader` (deterministic batching), does not run `ModuleValidator.fix`, warm-starts from a public pretrained checkpoint, and reports the accountant's (ε, δ) as the privacy claim on the whole training pipeline. Per chapter 04, which of the chapter's six operational failure modes are hit, and what is the engineering fix for each?

- A. None — the Opacus wrapper handles everything.
- B. Only `ModuleValidator` — the other two are fine.
- C. Three of the six failure modes are hit. (i) **Non-Poisson batching** — the accountant assumes Poisson sampling; a standard `DataLoader` violates the assumption and silently invalidates the (ε, δ) bound. Fix: use Opacus's `DPDataLoader` (or `make_private_with_epsilon`'s sample-rate parameter). (ii) **Layer replacement not applied** — BatchNorm and some LayerNorm paths bypass per-example clipping if `ModuleValidator.fix` is skipped. Fix: run `ModuleValidator.validate(model)` and `ModuleValidator.fix(model)` before training. (iii) **Warm-starting from a non-DP checkpoint** — the pretrained model carries whatever it memorised; the (ε, δ) applies only to the *fine-tune step*, not the end-to-end pipeline. Fix: state the warm-start explicitly in the privacy claim ("ε applies to the fine-tune only"), or train from scratch. Each failure gets a pass / fail entry in the release artefact's checklist.
- D. Failure modes are theoretical and do not matter in practice.

## 9. A level-25 report cites "DP-SGD ε = 3, δ = 10⁻⁵" without naming an accountant, computes record-level neighbouring on an LLM fine-tune where single customers contribute thousands of tokens across many conversations, and uses the record-level ε as the user-level privacy claim. Per chapter 04, what are the two specific things wrong?

- A. Nothing — ε = 3 is a small enough number regardless.
- B. The team should have used ε = 1 to be safer.
- C. Two things wrong. First, **(ε, δ) must name the accountant** — chapter 04 is explicit that ε is not read off σ but is produced by a composition analysis, and the state of practice in Opacus and TF Privacy is an RDP accountant, with GDP / PRV as alternatives; citing ε without the accountant means the number is not reproducible and is not comparable across runs. Second, **record-level DP-SGD understates user-level privacy when subjects contribute many records** — chapter 04 calls out LLM fine-tunes on customer conversations as the specific shape where this breaks, and the correct engineering move is either user-level DP-SGD (McMahan et al. 2018) or an explicit group-privacy adjustment on the record-level ε. The shippable claim names the accountant, the sampling assumption, and the "neighbouring" definition.
- D. User-level DP is a level-30 concern, not a level-25 one.

## 10. A team deploys Microsoft Presidio only on the model's outputs, redacting PII before responses leave the system, and does not run Presidio at the ingest path or inside the RAG retrieval loop. The extraction eval from chapter 03 finds that fine-tuned PII is being emitted on direct-completion prompts. Per chapter 05, what is the chapter's framing of this failure mode, and what are the three pipeline positions Presidio must cover?

- A. Nothing — an output filter is sufficient.
- B. The team should swap Presidio for a different library.
- C. Chapter 05 names this as the first common failure mode: **"Presidio as a single output-side filter."** The output filter is the *last line of defence*, not the whole defence — PII that entered training and is emitted on direct-completion prompts bypasses the output filter's semantic model for "do not quote the retrieved context" because the string is coming out of the model's parameters, not out of a retrieved chunk. Chapter 05's shippable shape deploys Presidio in **three positions**: (i) **ingest** — before data enters training or a RAG store, the single largest leverage point for memorisation risk ("a string that never entered training cannot be memorised"); (ii) **retrieval** — after retrieval and before the chunk enters the prompt, with an access-control check on flagged entities; (iii) **output** — on the model's response, with a whitelist for the user's own PII and the authorised retrieved context. The three positions protect against different failure modes and are not substitutes.
- D. Chapter 05 does not require ingest-side redaction.

## 11. A team authors a custom Presidio recogniser for an internal order-number pattern (`ORD-[A-Z0-9]{4}-\d{6}`), sets the recogniser's `score` to `1.0`, and ships without a labelled test set "because the regex is tight enough." The team also reports Presidio's `PERSON` recognition on their English-formal customer-support corpus at 0.78 precision / 0.82 recall as the single pipeline number, and asserts this is the pipeline's precision / recall on the whole deployed system. Per chapter 05, what are the two specific things wrong?

- A. Nothing — a tight regex does not need calibration, and English numbers are universal.
- B. The team should use `score = 0.5` instead of `1.0`.
- C. Two things wrong. First, **custom recogniser score calibration is required, not optional** — chapter 05 is explicit that the `score` is the recogniser's asserted confidence and must be set against the labelled evaluation; `score = 1.0` without a test set is the red-flag pattern the chapter names, and every custom recogniser ships with a labelled test set of positive and negative examples pulled from real data. Second, **a single language / formality number hides the real envelope** — chapter 05 is explicit that the eval slice must be drawn from production-shape data and **sliced by language, domain, and formality** ("English-formal customer-support text is different from Japanese chat is different from OCR'd PDFs"); a report that cites one PERSON number as the pipeline's number is silently blind to the slices where Presidio's coverage is weakest. The shippable report calibrates `score` on a labelled test set and reports per-entity precision / recall / F1 per slice.
- D. Precision / recall are nice-to-have; what matters is coverage.

## 12. A privacy-risk report concludes with the summary sentence "the system is GDPR-compliant; DP-SGD and Presidio close the privacy risks." The report is being prepared for the risk council, the DPO, and (indirectly) external counsel. Per chapter 06, what are the two specific things wrong with this conclusion, and what is the shippable boundary the chapter draws?

- A. Nothing — if the controls are in place, GDPR compliance follows.
- B. The report should have said "GDPR-adjacent" instead.
- C. Two things wrong. First, **"GDPR-compliant" is a legal conclusion, not an engineering claim** — chapter 06 is explicit that the engineer does not interpret GDPR articles, decide lawful basis under Article 6, or draft the DPIA; counsel does. The shippable engineering sentence is "the technical controls listed in section 4 close the empirical risks measured in section 3 to the residual levels in section 5; the mapping to GDPR concepts is in section 7; the compliance conclusion is counsel's." Second, **the residual claim must be bounded on attack strength, time, and threat model** — "DP-SGD and Presidio close the privacy risks" is unqualified; no eval supports an unconditional claim. The shippable shape says "under attack A at configuration C, on evaluation dataset D, as of release R, under threat model T, the residual signal is X." Section 10 of the report also names what escalates to counsel (interpretation, lawful-basis decisions, DPIA authorship) and what escalates to `model-evaluation-engineer` at level 30 (confidence intervals, statistical-significance framing, distribution-shift robustness).
- D. The legal conclusion is the engineer's prerogative when the technical evidence is strong.

## Answer key

1. **C.** Chapter 01, "Motivation" and "The four attacks": privacy risk decomposes into four empirically distinct attacks (MIA, attribute inference, model inversion, memorisation / extraction) with their own evals and defences. Collapsing them into one sentence is how privacy incidents get missed at review; the shippable shape is four separate register rows with named attack evaluations and residual numbers.
2. **B.** Chapter 01, "Attack surface vs. deployment shape": the applicability table pins RAG-on-internal-documents-without-fine-tune as "not directly on the base model" for MIA, attribute inference, and inversion; the risk for this shape lives in the retrieval store (the chapter-05 PII-pipeline problem). The report files n/a rows with the applicability-table justification rather than silently omitting them.
3. **C.** Chapter 02, "Motivation", "Why AUC alone is misleading — the ROC-shape argument", and "LiRA — Likelihood Ratio Attack": AUC averages conceal the high-confidence tail that matters for disclosure risk; LiRA (Carlini S&P 2022) is designed to make the low-FPR tail visible; the shippable shape reports AUC alongside TPR at 1 % and 0.1 % FPR, per slice, with both Shokri and LiRA on the same table.
4. **C.** Chapter 02, "The reporting table this module files" and "How the number lands on the mod-103 register": MIA is not uniform across records; outlier deciles and small classes are the highest-vulnerability slices; the shippable register row carries overall and slice worst-case as two distinct residual cells. "The slice worst-case is reported alongside the overall number" is the chapter's exact framing.
5. **C.** Chapter 03, "Canary-based memorisation eval (recommended for level 25)" ("Prompt budget" and "Elicitation strategies attempted" as engineering choices to be explicit about) and "Residual-risk signal" (the shape of the curve and the "never 'no extraction possible'" discipline). The elicitation-strategy list must include at least direct completion, prefix-from-context, and divergence prompting.
6. **C.** Chapter 03, "Model inversion" (specifically "Why it matters" — "small-population classifiers … are inherently privacy-fragile: the 'class' is a person") and "Register row" (defences include "don't expose confidence vectors on production APIs — return top-1 label only"). The eval reports reconstruction quality *and* identifiability together.
7. **C.** Chapter 04, "Defending the (ε, δ) choice at review" (specifically the "ε = 100 is still DP" anti-pattern) and "Defending the (ε, δ) choice at review — 'We used DP so MIA can't happen'" anti-pattern. The (ε, δ) bound is a formal property of the mechanism, not a substitute for the empirical MIA eval, and ε = 100 is practically useless.
8. **C.** Chapter 04, "Operational failure modes that silently invalidate the privacy claim": non-Poisson batching, layer replacement not applied, and warm-starting from a non-DP checkpoint are three of the six named failure modes; each has a specific engineering fix (DP dataloader; `ModuleValidator.fix`; stating the warm-start in the privacy claim scope).
9. **C.** Chapter 04, "Privacy accounting: RDP and the Gaussian mechanism" ("Pin the accountant — which one, which version — and record the resulting (ε, δ)") and "User-level DP vs. record-level DP" (the LLM-fine-tune-on-customer-conversations example and the group-privacy adjustment). The shippable claim names the accountant, the sampling assumption, and the "neighbouring" definition.
10. **C.** Chapter 05, "Motivation" ("Presidio as a single output-side filter" as the first common failure mode) and "The three pipeline positions" (ingest, retrieval, output — each with its own protective purpose). Ingest-side redaction is the single largest leverage point for memorisation risk.
11. **C.** Chapter 05, "Custom recognisers — the pattern" ("Score calibration" and "Test set" as required discipline) and "False positives and false negatives — the accounting" ("Slice the eval by language, domain, and formality where the corpus supports it"). Custom recognisers calibrate `score` on a labelled test set; the eval is sliced by language / domain / formality.
12. **C.** Chapter 06, "The residual-risk claim the report is entitled to make" (attack strength / time / threat model as the three bounds; "the system is GDPR-compliant" named as an anti-pattern), "Boundary 1 — what is out of scope to legal counsel" (interpretation, Article 6 lawful basis, DPIA authorship), and "Boundary 2 — what escalates to `model-evaluation-engineer` (level 30)" (confidence intervals, statistical-significance framing, distribution-shift robustness).
